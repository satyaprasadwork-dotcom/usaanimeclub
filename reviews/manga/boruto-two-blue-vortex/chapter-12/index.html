Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 12 Critical Response: A Rescue Worth More Than Victory: Why the Thorn Soul Bulb Matters

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 12, Thorn Soul Bulb, critically examines Boruto and Sarada's teamwork against Hidari, Uzuhiko's limitation, Kawaki's intervention, the chance to save Sasuke and Jura's ambush.

Source-based commentary by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Chapter 12 finally gives the battle a prize that matters more than defeating an enemy: the possibility of restoring Sasuke. The teamwork is a welcome corrective to one-man power showcases, but Jura's sudden long-range interruption makes that hard-won progress painfully fragile.

The best reason to fight Hidari is not revenge

With Chapter 12, Boruto's dramatic return begins to acquire a clear practical purpose beyond impressing the village. He knows that the Divine Tree being shaped by Sasuke has left the real Sasuke trapped, and he believes there is an object connected with the creature that can restore the victim. The official Shueisha volume description establishes the simultaneous attacks by Jura and Hidari; post-release discussion identifies the object as the Thorn Soul Bulb. That knowledge changes what success should mean. Killing an enemy and saving a person are not necessarily the same action, and the difference gives the encounter a more difficult moral shape.

Sarada's presence makes that distinction concrete. Hidari carries something recognizable from her father without being her father. His dangerous attraction to her is not an expression of Sasuke's affection, yet the resemblance makes the prospect of destroying him emotionally complicated. For Boruto, the fight concerns a mentor whose sacrifice enabled his survival; for Sarada, it concerns a parent the village has been unable to reach. A victory that ends with a corpse and no recovery would leave the central wound open. The chapter's strongest idea is that the real objective is not to prove which new technique is superior, but to bring a specific person home.

Boruto and Sarada finally need each other in the same fight

Post-release accounts describe Boruto confronting Hidari, using a wind technique to force space before the enemy retreats through his Claw Marks. Sarada and the other defenders must remain alert as the creature shifts position and attacks from unexpected directions. Boruto prepares Uzuhiko but cannot simply unleash the fully developed technique without time. This creates the kind of tactical dependence an ensemble series needs: one character can prepare the decisive attack only if others prevent an interruption. Sarada's willingness to buy that time is therefore not incidental support. It is an actual condition of the plan.

This arrangement brings the action closer to the old Naruto principle that teams succeed by coordinating partial strengths rather than stacking individual transformations. The chapter does not need to make Sarada stronger than Hidari in a fair duel to give her a meaningful role. Evading an opponent, countering surprise attacks and creating an opening can be just as consequential as dealing the final blow. It would be premature to claim the story has solved all concerns about Sarada's agency. The larger arc still uses rescues repeatedly, and Boruto remains a disproportionately capable protagonist. But for this installment, the fight works better because success depends on several people doing different things rather than watching a single prodigy perform.

Uzuhiko becomes more interesting when timing is a constraint

The earlier debut of Rasengan Uzuhiko risked creating a simple solution to Code and anyone comparable to him. Chapter 12 makes the technique more dramatic by establishing a practical problem: Boruto has to draw on an extraordinary force, and the strongest application needs preparation. He cannot deploy it instantly under every battlefield condition. When Hidari creates pressure through his unpredictable movements, the timing becomes more important than the raw size of the attack. A technique whose effect is nearly overwhelming is still vulnerable if a combatant never gets the opportunity to use it.

That is a more useful limitation than announcing that each successive enemy has a numerically higher power level. Restrictions turn abilities into decisions. Does Boruto save the accumulated strength for an ideal moment, or release a less prepared version because Sarada is at risk? Contemporary retellings describe him choosing the latter, damaging Hidari severely enough for Kawaki to finish the attacker. The scene makes the character prioritize another person over perfect execution. It also gives Kawaki a genuinely helpful battlefield contribution after several installments in which his ability to challenge the new villains looked doubtful. The two may still despise each other's aims, but an enemy does not care about the labels that divide them.

The Thorn Soul Bulb changes a fight into a rescue mission

After Hidari is defeated, the object that remains creates a sudden shift in the chapter's emotional direction. Boruto explains that the Thorn Soul Bulb is connected to returning Sasuke from his tree-bound state. An object small enough to be carried becomes more important than the explosive exchange that produced it. That contrast is elegantly simple. The Divine Trees were born from victims; restoring a victim may require confronting and defeating a manifestation born from that person's chakra. What looked like a monster-fighting arc becomes a recovery problem with a potentially terrible cost for every person still imprisoned.

The implication extends beyond Sasuke without guaranteeing every rescue will follow identical rules. Moegi has been trapped, and the fate of the Sand Village has not yet been fully explained. If the same kind of item can help others, the story acquires a common objective that antagonists and protectors may interpret differently. But this installment is not a complete medical guide to the phenomenon. Readers should not assume that holding the bulb automatically completes a restoration or that the risk of using it has been fully described. Its power in the story is the possibility it represents to Sarada and Boruto after years of apparently irreversible loss.

Jura's ambush is a sharp reminder that success is local

Boruto has barely obtained that hope before Jura attacks from a distance, striking him and retrieving the object. Sarada's attempt to intervene also places her in danger. The reversal is brutal because the preceding battle has been framed as a difficult but earned achievement. The protagonists developed a plan, worked together, overcame Hidari and briefly possessed something that could help Sasuke. One opponent who has not been neutralized can make all that effort uncertain again. The new Divine Trees are not staged as a series of polite single-combat encounters; they coordinate, observe and exploit a vulnerable moment.

There is a risk that a sudden ranged attack feels less like a satisfying complication than the author taking back a victory to prolong the story. Jura's capabilities have already been established, however, and the danger is consistent with his willingness to treat people as interesting obstacles rather than partners. The important consequence is not simply that Boruto gets hurt. It is that the main threat now knows he understands more about the Thorn Soul system than expected. Knowledge itself has become dangerous: an ally able to restore victims threatens the Divine Trees' continued autonomy, whether or not any individual battle can be won.

Verdict: the arc discovers an objective worth caring about

Thorn Soul Bulb is among the most purposeful chapters in the early Two Blue Vortex run. Sarada's contribution to the fight is concrete, Uzuhiko's charge time provides a meaningful limitation, and the object recovered after Hidari's defeat gives the conflict a human goal beyond revenge or rankings. The prospect of recovering Sasuke is emotionally intelligible to readers who remember the bond he built with Boruto and the father Sarada has been trying to save. The twist is more persuasive because the win is contested by an already dangerous enemy rather than undone by a brand-new power invented on the spot.

The cost is that the rapid handoff from apparent rescue to crisis gives little time for the characters to absorb either relief or grief. The series continues to favor high-impact reversals over sustained emotional conversation, and that may frustrate readers who want to see Sarada process her father's situation. The underlying tension remains substantial: can the protagonists defeat a creature without losing their chance to rescue the person inside its story? The analysis is an independently written, source-backed critical response informed by Shueisha's volume record and contemporary after-release commentary. It is not a panel-by-panel firsthand art review and does not pretend the full later mechanics were established in Chapter 12.
