Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 32 Review: Daemon's Reflection Has a Limit—and the Plan Depends on Everyone Respecting Theirs

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 32, Dancing with the Devil, analyzed: Mamushi swarming Eida's location, Daemon's palm-contact condition, Sarada's premature Ohirume, Kawaki overheating and the collapse of Shikamaru's planned finishing sequence.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Daemon is frightening because his protection of Eida appears nearly absolute, but the chapter reveals constraints without making him useless. The larger lesson belongs to Sarada and Kawaki: a carefully coordinated plan can fail when the strongest members exceed their limits.

Moving the swarm is more sensible than chasing it

The plan inherited from Chapter 31 is simple enough to explain but difficult to execute. Rather than let every Mamushi copy appear in a separate district, Shikamaru intends to lead them toward Eida's location. There the defenders can concentrate their strongest techniques and reduce the enemy to a number Sarada might be able to eliminate in one calculated use of Ohirume. The operation recognizes that Mamushi's multiplication is a logistical challenge. Defeating a copy has limited value while other copies continue moving through the village. Concentrating them creates the possibility of a decisive result, but it also concentrates danger around the person they want to consume.

The plan's morality therefore depends on whether Eida's participation and protection are treated seriously. Her Senrigan may expose her to Mamushi, and her brother is willing to use extraordinary force to keep the clones away. The series has repeatedly shown how little control Eida has over the attention that powerful beings direct toward her. Being a lure in a plan agreed to by others can be another form of being denied an ordinary choice. The chapter does not need to make every defender into a villain to acknowledge that problem. It only needs to let Eida's safety matter as something more than a tool for assembling the enemy.

Daemon's secret condition is a welcome restraint on a monstrous advantage

Daemon finally has to face a swarm large enough to make even his powers an uncomfortable responsibility. Contemporary accounts explain that his Reflection ability requires both palms to be in contact with another person. This is a useful clarification after years in which the ability could appear to punish any attack without an observable condition. He remains exceptionally fast and strong, but Reflection cannot simply be described as a passive shield that operates under every imaginable circumstance. Contact matters, and the opponent may eventually learn to interfere with the condition.

The limitation creates questions rather than answering all of them. The chapter demonstrates the importance of contact, but it does not establish a complete list of which intentions and techniques Reflection affects. A restraining maneuver or a tactic that deprives Daemon of the necessary contact might behave differently from a direct attempt to kill him. Those possibilities are not confirmed results. The correct critical approach is to recognize the observed weakness and resist transforming it into a guaranteed way to defeat him. The distinction matters in an online debate where one detail about a power can rapidly become a definitive claim that every other fictional character could exploit.

Mamushi's copies are numerous, hungry and progressively less thoughtful

The swarm's scale becomes its own liability. Earlier explanations suggested that Mamushi's multiplication preserves dangerous combat capability while eroding cognitive performance. This installment shows the effect under pressure. The copies are overwhelmingly focused on reaching Eida, and they have difficulty producing a coherent answer to Daemon despite possessing some understanding of the abilities inherited from Bug's experiences. They can reproduce threats faster than a normal group of enemies, yet growing numbers do not necessarily produce a coordinated strategy. That trade-off gives the defenders a reason to attempt the concentrated fight at all.

The apparent simplicity of the copies should not make the scene feel safe. There are many of them, they can continue draining the defenders' energy, and the city is not a neutral arena where every attack can be used without collateral damage. An enemy who is individually less intelligent may still be impossible to contain through ordinary tactics. This is where the chapter's pressure comes from: the heroes have found a battlefield where the enemy is predictable, but their own resources are limited. Success requires timing rather than simply outthinking a single rival.

Sarada's mistake has implications beyond her chakra

The defenders intend to weaken the swarm before Sarada deploys Ohirume against the remaining copies. Contemporary post-release criticism describes her using the technique earlier than the strategy requires while already low on chakra. The attack is impressive but leaves her unable to perform the intended final action. This is the chapter's most frustrating decision because the price of her power was established in the preceding installment. Sarada wants to become Hokage, and a leader's duty includes reporting when a crucial role is beyond their current capacity. An avoidable error in estimating stamina can endanger teammates even when the initial impulse is to protect people.

The scene has dramatic legitimacy if the story treats the mistake as a mistake. Sarada should be able to take risks and fail; being a capable character does not require always choosing correctly. The issue is whether she learns to communicate her limits and share responsibility rather than being exhausted whenever the plot needs somebody else to arrive. Her Mangekyō is important because it can influence the battlefield, but the potential cost to her eyesight and stamina should encourage deliberate planning. There is room for a richer character arc if she learns that ambition and mutual dependence are not opposites.

Kawaki has firepower but cannot carry the rest of the operation alone

When Sarada can no longer fulfill her expected role, Kawaki tries to make up the difference. The fight reveals that his altered body can overheat, reinforcing the trade-off established when he demanded a more aggressive configuration from Amado. His offense matters, but the upgrade is not an unlimited supply of safe attacks. That keeps the fight from invalidating the earlier concern that new power would have a cost. A person with Kawaki's ability to produce explosive violence may still be the wrong solution when the goal is to methodically contain thousands of attackers.

The scene also gives him a legitimate tactical place without resolving his moral relationship with Boruto. Their temporary cooperation against Jura made sense because both needed to survive. Here the objective concerns the protection of the village and its civilians, not a sudden reunion of brothers who have forgiven everything. Kawaki's weakness does not make him a coward, just as Sarada's exhaustion does not erase her progress. Both demonstrate that extreme output can become dangerous when a team cannot maintain the planned sequence.

The most alarming detail is that the strategy can work and still fail

Shikamaru's plan is not inherently foolish. Gathering a swarm that repeatedly seeks Eida, eliminating the copies in stages and reserving a large-area technique for the final group is an intelligible solution to a complex threat. But a good strategy requires accurate information about the people expected to execute it. If Sarada has already used too much chakra and Kawaki can only fight at maximum output briefly, the mathematical target of reducing the horde to a manageable number becomes unrealistic. The failure is not caused solely by Mamushi being stronger. It is caused by insufficient reserve capacity in the defensive plan.

Daemon is also tiring under relentless attack, a particularly effective way to remind readers that his ability does not remove the ordinary limitations of the person using it. The defenders are not defeated in a conventional duel; they are being worn down by a foe whose individual copies are expendable. The chapter gains suspense by respecting this asymmetry. A few more victories against the swarm are not necessarily progress when every fighter grows weaker while the enemy keeps reproducing.

Verdict: the best power reveal is a restriction

Dancing with the Devil makes Daemon's Reflection more narratively useful by identifying a condition that helps the reader understand when he is protected. The swarm's cognitive weakness also preserves the possibility of a tactical answer rather than reducing Mamushi to an unstoppable wall of bodies. What turns the story toward disaster, however, is the mismatch between a carefully constructed group plan and the limits of the people executing it. Sarada overextends, Kawaki overheats and even Daemon begins feeling the cost of an extended defense.

The weaknesses are serious. Sarada's premature use of Ohirume risks repeating a pattern of sidelining her after a showcase, and some familiar adults remain conspicuously absent from the largest battle Konoha has faced in years. The battle's numerical escalation also threatens to become monotonous. Yet the overall point is sound: strength without restraint is an unreliable foundation for collective security. The chapter deliberately stops short of the resolution that follows in Chapter 33.
