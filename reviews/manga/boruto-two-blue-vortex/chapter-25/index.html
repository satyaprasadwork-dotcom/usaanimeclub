Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 25 Review: Those Girls Finds Hope in Recovery—and a Terrifying Bargain With Momoshiki

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 25, Those Girls, examined: Moegi's restoration, Himawari and Kurama's training, Sarada and Sumire's reconciliation, the uncertainty around Yodo and Boruto's offer to Momoshiki.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

The first real recovery after the Divine Tree crisis gives this chapter emotional space. Sarada and Sumire choose solidarity over rivalry, but Boruto's private willingness to trade his own body for victory makes the quiet aftermath more disturbing than a battle.

A recovery changes the meaning of the previous battles

Those Girls begins by giving the preceding mission something more important than another victory pose. Konohamaru and Team 10 use the Thorn Soul item to release Moegi from the tree that imprisoned her, and medical support confirms that she needs time to recover. Shueisha's Volume 7 description independently establishes that the team has returned after the defeat of Matsuri and Ryū. For several chapters, the protagonists have risked their lives for the possibility that a Divine Tree's defeat might free a person. The chapter finally tests that hope in a recognizable human setting: friends gathering around someone who has survived and medical professionals assessing the damage.

The success matters because it prevents the entire arc from becoming an endless parade of threatening manifestations. The Thorn Soul Bulb is not just a reward dropped by a powerful enemy. It represents a route back to an ordinary relationship. Konohamaru spent the fight wrestling with the resemblance between Matsuri and his childhood friend; now the difference between the creature and Moegi is no longer a purely abstract problem. Saving Moegi means the people whom the Divine Trees took can return, even when the experience may have consequences after their release.

That hopeful outcome also exposes the importance of pacing. A major rescue deserves more than a quick acknowledgment before the story moves on. Moegi's own response, her experience of confinement and the effect on Team 10 could become important later. If the series treats her merely as a body recovered and then forgets her, the emotional sacrifice required to obtain the item will feel thinner in retrospect. A responsible critical response should celebrate the progress while asking whether the restored person will get to matter in the story that follows.

The Sand Village's relief does not answer every unresolved loss

Contemporary accounts describe Shikamaru receiving reassuring news about Gaara and Shinki. That provides an answer to the fear created when Shinki trapped himself while protecting his father. It also connects the Sand Village mission to the wider political work Konoha must do in the next arc. The operation was dangerous and based on incomplete intelligence, yet it achieved the result the younger fighters were trying to secure. There is understandable relief in knowing the victims can be returned rather than permanently sacrificed so a new antagonist can exist.

The remaining uncertainty is Yodo's condition. Post-release commentators note the absence of a clear update in this chapter and express concern that she may have died after the battle. Silence is not a formal death announcement. An editorial review should not turn the character's missing status into confirmed fact, even while criticizing the way the story has handled her injury. She fought for Shinki and her companions, and the consequences of her actions deserve a meaningful response. The story has already used her suffering as part of the pressure surrounding Sarada's Mangekyō. Its obligation to Yodo is not discharged simply because the more famous characters have survived.

This is an issue of narrative attention rather than a demand that every character must be safe. A dangerous fight can permanently harm someone without poor writing. The problem arises when a person's fate is made less important than the emotional reaction it produces in somebody else. Chapter 25 provides enough quiet space to acknowledge unresolved damage; whether the series chooses to use that space is a measure of how seriously it takes its supporting cast.

Sarada and Sumire refuse the easiest kind of romantic conflict

The conversation between Sarada and Sumire gives the title a clear immediate meaning. Sarada admits feelings she has been struggling to name, and Sumire is honest about her own longstanding affection for Boruto. The significant outcome is that they do not turn their friendship into a contest to humiliate the other person. They acknowledge the difficulty and retain the possibility of cooperating to help somebody who remains separated from his home. For a series that has often framed women's relationships through their ties to male protagonists, this is a welcome choice. Jealousy does not have to be a character's final response to another person's vulnerability.

That does not make the scene beyond criticism. Both young women have identities that extend well past the question of whom Boruto might eventually love. Sarada wants to become Hokage, is trying to restore her father and has just won a dangerous battle through a unique power. Sumire has experience in scientific research, complex knowledge of the village's security and a genuine capacity to think critically about the effect of Omnipotence. If their entire reconciliation is measured only by a promise not to compete destructively over Boruto, the manga has still allowed a boy's romantic future to organize their emotional lives.

The better interpretation is that they are negotiating honesty and solidarity under circumstances that make both difficult. Sarada no longer has to pretend her feelings are evidence of weakness. Sumire can express disappointment without defining herself by it. Their friendship will become more convincing if the next chapters allow the two to disagree about practical decisions as well as emotional matters, and to help each other in fields where Boruto is not the central subject.

Sakura's presence highlights the absence of a larger family conversation

Moegi's recovery also places Sakura in a medical role, providing reassurance about the effects of being trapped inside a tree. She is one of the few surviving adults whose long history directly connects the original Naruto generation to the current crisis. Yet her involvement raises a question readers have good reason to ask: how does she understand what has happened to Sasuke, her husband, who remains trapped in another tree? The manga has given Sarada several moments of personal distress about her father but has not devoted equivalent space to a family discussion between mother and daughter.

The criticism is not that Sakura must solve Sasuke's situation instantly. The character has responsibilities to injured people in the village, and she may lack knowledge that Boruto and Koji possess. But the absence of conversation becomes noticeable when the story pauses long enough to describe the likely recovery of another victim. A scene of Sakura recognizing the practical possibilities for Sasuke—or openly admitting what she does not know—would make the rescue feel more consequential across the ensemble.

That is a missed opportunity specific to the story's generational theme. The previous generation is not merely a source of techniques and inherited chakra; its members are people who still experience loss. An adult who once fought alongside Naruto and Sasuke should have perspectives on the new crisis that a younger fighter cannot reproduce. The manga has built an excellent reason to revisit those bonds, and Chapter 25 only begins to show what it might mean.

Himawari's training is more compelling than another instant awakening

While the older fighters process the Sand mission, Himawari begins working with the young Kurama within their shared internal space. Contemporary accounts describe Kurama emphasizing chakra control rather than promising that another sudden surge of power will solve everything. This is a healthier development than treating her initial awakening as a complete ability she has only to remember during a desperate fight. She knows the chakra exists, and she has seen what happens when she cannot manage it. Training gives her an opportunity to make choices before the next emergency rather than only in response to someone being hurt.

The relationship also offers a striking contrast with Boruto's experience of Momoshiki. Kurama and Himawari can cooperate toward greater understanding even while some details about their unusual compatibility remain unexplained. Boruto carries a powerful presence whose goals are incompatible with his freedom. The siblings' parallel situations should not be simplified into a claim that the powers have identical rules or moral qualities. Their shared condition creates different questions about control, responsibility and how inherited strength affects a young person's sense of self.

At this stage, Kurama's remarks about Himawari's unusual nature should be reported with care. It may be significant that her bond seems different from a conventional jinchūriki relationship, but the chapter does not provide a complete, testable theory of her biology. The best point is the character direction: a girl who entered the conflict as an unsuspecting target is now actively learning how to protect herself and others. She does not need to become a smaller copy of Naruto for that growth to matter.

The real cliffhanger is Boruto offering himself to Momoshiki

The quietest and most disturbing conversation occurs inside Boruto's own consciousness. Contemporary reports describe Momoshiki no longer possessing the same easy opportunity to take control of his host, and expressing bitterness about the choices that left him bound to Boruto. The power struggle has changed since the original series, but it has not disappeared. Boruto approaches him with a proposal tied to the threat posed by Jura: cooperation now, in exchange for the possibility of full control later. The offer is not an ordinary alliance. It involves the ownership of a living person's body and the fate of everyone who might face Momoshiki afterward.

This is a far more alarming choice than an additional desperate jutsu. The preceding battle proved that Boruto and Kawaki could escape Jura with a plan, but that does not mean they can defeat him or prevent a future catastrophe. Boruto appears willing to gamble his own autonomy to gain a chance of saving other lives. The logic resembles the decisions Koji previously weighed about Yodo, but with an important difference: Boruto is considering sacrificing something that belongs to him. Even that distinction has limits, because giving Momoshiki control would expose other people to danger they did not consent to accept.

The scene gives the chapter its true tension. A young man trained for years to avoid being possessed is contemplating an arrangement that might make his survival more dangerous than his death. This cannot be presented as a clever shortcut whose ethical costs will vanish when the next opponent appears. It is a high-stakes promise involving somebody who has repeatedly sought power over Boruto's life. The story must eventually establish the terms, the safeguards—if any—and whether Momoshiki has any reason to honor the deal.

Verdict: the aftermath earns its space, then threatens a new disaster

Those Girls is valuable because it refuses to jump directly from the fight against Jura to another battle. Moegi is restored, news about Shinki and Gaara offers relief, Sarada and Sumire can speak without immediate danger, and Himawari begins the unglamorous work of learning control. Those events are not distractions from the action. They are evidence of why the action mattered. The chapter's title draws attention to characters whose friendships and decisions could otherwise be swallowed by a story focused almost entirely on Boruto and Kawaki.

Its weaknesses remain significant. Yodo's fate deserves clearer attention, Sakura has little opportunity to confront the implications for Sasuke, and the conversation between Sarada and Sumire risks leaving them defined primarily by romantic attachment. Yet the final bargain with Momoshiki turns those quieter human scenes into a reminder of what may be lost next. Boruto's commitment to protecting others is genuine; the method he now contemplates could undermine the freedom he has been struggling to recover.
