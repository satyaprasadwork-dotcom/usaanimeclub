Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 35 Review: Jura's Affection for Hidari Changes What the Divine Trees May Become

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 35, Cute, examined: Jura recognizing sadness over Hidari, Sarada agreeing to Boruto's dangerous plan involving Code, the risk to her new Mangekyō, and Amado's attempt to seize control of Koji's future knowledge.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Cute makes emotion a source of new danger on both sides. Jura cares about Hidari in a way he did not care about the fallen Divine Trees, Boruto trusts Sarada with a risky plan, and Amado treats Koji's agency as a technical obstacle.

Jura's question is no longer whether love exists

Cute is an unexpectedly gentle title for a chapter whose threats include Code, the Divine Trees and the scientist who has repeatedly tried to control other people's bodies. Its opening provides the key. Jura tells Hidari that Mamushi has been lost, and their conversation turns to the difference between his treatment of Hidari and the other creatures. In earlier encounters, Jura showed little interest in rescuing Ryū or Matsuri even when their existence was threatened. He did, however, secure Hidari's Thorn Soul Bulb and restore him. As contemporary post-release analysis explains, the distinction is emotional: the thought of a world without Hidari made Jura sad.

The admission reverses several of Jura's previous arguments about human attachment. He studied books and people as though love were a curious mechanism that caused less rational beings to take unreasonable risks. Matsuri's concern for Konohamaru offered one example, and Boruto's willingness to remain in danger for his friends offered another. Jura could describe the behavior but was reluctant to recognize its value. Now his own decisions have been shaped by the possibility of losing somebody whose company matters to him. The logical contradiction is not a mistake. It is a reason his character has become more interesting.

The chapter does not declare him good, and it would be misleading to treat sadness as proof of redemption. Someone can care intensely for one person while remaining indifferent to the suffering of many others. Jura's selective attachment may even make him more dangerous. If he is willing to restore Hidari but not his other companions, his behavior becomes less predictable from the standpoint of the other Divine Trees. The distinction creates a genuinely uncertain relationship between new emotions and existing goals.

Hidari is not Sasuke, even when the resemblance matters

Hidari emerged from the chakra and circumstances associated with Sasuke, and his continued existence places Sarada's family at risk. It is understandable that readers will interpret his interactions with Jura through familiar relationships from the earlier Naruto series. There are suggestive parallels in the way the new creatures depend on each other and in the emotional language Jura begins to use. Those associations can be productive without becoming a license to announce that Hidari is literally Sasuke or that Jura is secretly Naruto in a different body. The chapter establishes the creatures' present behavior rather than confirming a one-to-one romantic or psychological identity.

The distinction is especially important because Sasuke is still trapped. Hidari's existence is a threat to him and to Sarada, not a complete replacement for a person with decades of memories and relationships. A critic can discuss the emotional resonance of the pairing without denying the urgency of rescuing the original captive. If Jura has developed the ability to grieve, that does not erase the people whose lives have been damaged by the system that created him and his companions.

Hidari also needs individual agency. If he becomes only the object of Jura's newfound affection, his own motives will fade behind the more philosophical antagonist. The series has made the Divine Trees effective partly by giving each a distinctive curiosity or appetite. A strong continuation will allow Hidari's attachment to Sasuke's life and his new bond with Jura to create conflicting decisions rather than merely give Jura somebody to worry about.

Eida's observation reminds us that emotions are not private here

Eida's reaction to the conversation is revealing. She has spent much of the series looking for relationships that are not forced by her own charm, while also possessing an extraordinary ability to observe people at a distance. Watching Jura acknowledge feelings for Hidari presents a version of affection that emerged under circumstances neither creature seems to have planned. Eida can be surprised by it, but her position as an observer also creates a familiar problem: understanding another person's feelings is not equivalent to receiving their permission to watch.

That complication is part of the series' broader interest in surveillance. Koji studies possible futures, Eida sees distant events, and Amado uses technical knowledge to alter other people's choices. Every one of these capacities can be justified as useful for preventing disaster. Each also risks allowing the informed person to decide that others do not deserve control over information about their own lives. Eida's interest in an unexpected emotional development is relatively harmless compared with the invasion of privacy she has sometimes enabled, but the parallel helps explain why the manga keeps returning to the distinction between knowledge and consent.

Boruto finally asks Sarada to participate rather than simply trust him

The chapter's other major thread concerns Code. Koji has warned of possible futures in which the villain targets Sarada to force Boruto into a confrontation with Hidari and Jura. Rather than continuing to conceal every detail from his friend, Boruto explains the danger and asks her to participate in a risky plan. Contemporary accounts describe him proposing that she allow Code to seize her so Boruto can follow through a teleportation marker and reach the enemy's location. The plan could create a rare opportunity to approach the remaining Divine Trees and perhaps move closer to rescuing Sasuke.

The important improvement is that Sarada receives enough information to make a choice. For much of Two Blue Vortex, Boruto has acted on secret forecasts and left the people closest to him trying to interpret his sudden arrivals and disappearances. Sharing the outline of a plan restores some of the agency his secrecy has denied her. Sarada knows her father is trapped and has her own reasons to confront Hidari. She is not a parcel that the protagonist must retrieve once a villain takes it; she is a participant in a dangerous arrangement with stakes she can evaluate.

That does not settle every ethical concern. Agreeing to a kidnapping-shaped trap is an extraordinary risk, particularly for somebody whose eyesight and stamina have been damaged by repeated Mangekyō use. Boruto still controls information about his possible futures and his bargain with Momoshiki that may affect her judgment. Full trust would require acknowledging not only what Code might do but what Boruto may be unable to do if the plan fails. The chapter therefore offers a step toward honest cooperation rather than a completed model of it.

A risky plan becomes credible only when failure has been considered

The proposal to follow Code through Sarada depends on several assumptions: the villain must choose the predicted kind of attack, the teleportation marker must remain available, and Boruto must be able to act before the captive is taken somewhere from which escape is impossible. Earlier chapters established that his version of Flying Thunder God requires identification and concentration. Those constraints make his reliance on a marker meaningful. The plan is not guaranteed simply because Boruto has used teleportation successfully in other scenes.

It is also vulnerable to the central weakness of acting on Koji's information. A person who knows a likely ambush can change the choices that bring it about. Code might react to the presence of a marker, choose a different route or decide not to take Sarada at all. The plan could succeed in reaching the enemy while failing to protect the person who agreed to serve as bait. A responsible assessment should distinguish what the chapter proposes from what the later battle will actually establish. The trap and its final outcome do not belong to the same installment merely because they form a continuous storyline.

The strongest version of this thread is one in which Sarada is allowed to make adjustments when the situation differs from Boruto's instructions. She has already demonstrated that her abilities can transform a fight, but the cost of overusing them is real. An informed partner should be free to refuse a disastrous step rather than remain committed because a prediction called it necessary.

Amado is preparing a threat more intimate than another Divine Tree

While Boruto and Sarada assess Code, Amado moves toward a different source of extraordinary power: Kashin Koji's Prescience. Contemporary coverage describes him building technological tools, including imitations of Koji's small toads, and using the connections among Kawaki and Inojin to seek access to the hidden strategist. He also develops a means of adjusting Kawaki's offensive and defensive configuration more flexibly. These inventions demonstrate how effectively he can work with information others have revealed while believing their private arrangements remain secure.

Amado's interest in Koji is understandable from the perspective of a scientist determined to restore his daughter Akebi. If one of his creations can observe possible futures, the ability might tell him whether his plan is doomed before he spends further years arranging it. But the desire to learn does not grant the right to seize or alter a person against their will. Koji has himself been willing to manipulate other people for the sake of a predicted result, and the irony is sharp: the strategist may discover how little protection his own secret knowledge offers once somebody with different technical advantages begins treating him as a research subject.

The chapter lays the groundwork for Amado's more direct intervention. It should not be credited with every step or outcome that the next installment reveals. His intentions and preparations are visible here; the full consequences of the coercive operation belong to the following chapter. Preserving that boundary makes the story's pacing easier to follow and avoids a common problem with retrospective chapter reviews: turning a setup chapter into a summary of events that have not happened yet.

Why the title works better than another battlefield escalation

The word Cute could describe the surprising intimacy of Jura's newly acknowledged emotions, but it also highlights a contradiction that runs through the chapter. These are beings with staggering power who are struggling to understand an ordinary human experience. Jura's sadness would seem unremarkable in a different context; here it might change the behavior of someone capable of ending countless lives. The contrast between the small emotional word and the enormous consequences gives the installment a distinct identity after the exhausting Mamushi invasion.

The title also invites an uncomfortable comparison with Amado. Jura is newly learning why a particular person's continued existence matters to him. Amado has long understood love for his daughter and repeatedly used that love to justify controlling other people. One character is discovering attachment, while the other has allowed attachment to become an excuse for coercion. The manga's interest in feelings therefore extends beyond whether emotions make fighters stronger or weaker. It asks how a powerful person behaves after deciding another life is precious.

Verdict: a quieter chapter that redefines the next conflict

Cute works by arranging three different threats around relationships. Jura has discovered an attachment to Hidari that may complicate his choices. Boruto begins treating Sarada as a participant in a dangerous plan rather than someone to be protected entirely through secrecy. Amado prepares to approach Koji's future knowledge as a resource he intends to control. These threads share a question: when does caring about an outcome become a reason to override another person's freedom? The answer will be tested in the next stage of the story, but the setup has genuine tension.

The weaknesses are its reliance on extended discussion after an already talk-heavy arc, its use of a risky bait plan for a character still recovering from a powerful technique, and the convenience of Amado developing new technology precisely when the plot needs another complication. Those issues are worth recording without confusing future events with this chapter's confirmed developments. The installment earns attention by showing that an enemy's sadness can become as consequential as a new jutsu.
