Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 7 Review: Boruto Gives Mitsuki a Choice His Memories Cannot Explain

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 7, The Whereabouts of the Sun, examined: Sage Mode, Mitsuki's moon-and-sun identity, Boruto's restraint, and the complicated meaning of autonomy after Omnipotence.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

The combat is deliberately unequal, but the emotional argument is substantial. Rather than demanding Mitsuki return to him, Boruto invites his old teammate to become someone who can shine without choosing a single person to orbit.

A fight whose result matters less than its conversation

Chapter 7 pays off the pursuit set up at the end of Three Years. Mitsuki attacks Boruto with Sage Mode techniques, but this is not structured like the decisive confrontation of equally matched rivals. Published criticism describes Boruto avoiding the pressure without the panic or retaliation Mitsuki expects. The official Naruto retrospective independently confirms that Mitsuki is unable to bring himself to harm Boruto, and that the exchange turns toward the meaning of his supposed sun. The battle is therefore a vehicle for emotional investigation. The reader is watching somebody confront a contradiction between what he believes he remembers and what his responses to another person suggest.

That is both the strength and a danger of the scene. Action readers have waited years for Mitsuki's full abilities to occupy a serious conflict, and a confrontation that leaves him comparatively overmatched may feel unsatisfying. If the story only uses him to advertise how much Boruto has improved, it diminishes a character built around extraordinary potential. But the clash works as an emotional event because Mitsuki's strength cannot answer the question Boruto is asking. His anxiety is not just about an invader. It is about an entire self-concept that may have been attached to the wrong person, while the need for a guiding figure persists even when the identity of that person changes.

The sun and moon metaphor is finally tested

Long before the time skip, Mitsuki imagined himself in relation to Boruto as the moon to a sun. The official Naruto site documents how that language structured his loyalty across Team Seven's early missions. In Chapter 7, the metaphor is forced into a genuine dilemma: if Kawaki is his sun, why does the relationship fail to give Mitsuki the clarity and warmth he believes it should? Boruto does not offer a satisfying factual proof that erases the consequences of Omnipotence. He instead names the possibility that Mitsuki has already been sensing a mismatch for some time. The distinction between a memory and the feeling of living inside a relationship becomes the chapter's central subject.

This is far richer than simply telling readers that Boruto is the true sun and Kawaki is the false one. If Mitsuki were nothing more than a loyal satellite assigned to the correct boy, the problem would be solved by switching labels. The series instead has an opportunity to question the metaphor itself. Why must a person who can fight, make decisions and form attachments believe that his ability to shine belongs to somebody else? The image of a moon is moving because it suggests reflected light, but it can also confine a character to permanent dependence. Chapter 7 gains emotional maturity by letting that confinement become part of the problem.

Boruto's restraint is a form of respect

The clearest contribution of the official Mitsuki retrospective is that Boruto tells his former teammate he is capable of shining on his own. He also leaves open the possibility that Mitsuki may seek him out when ready. Those words do not make him a flawless friend. Boruto has withheld information, disappeared for years, and returned in circumstances that make honest conversation nearly impossible. Yet his refusal to demand immediate obedience is meaningful. He recognizes that an identity constructed under false memories cannot be repaired by another person issuing instructions, even when those instructions come from the man who knows the original history.

That respect distinguishes this interaction from the more severe relationships elsewhere in the narrative. Kawaki often frames protection as control; Mitsuki himself has treated loyalty as a reason to threaten Boruto. The protagonist responds by refusing to turn familiarity into a claim of ownership. He does not say that their earlier friendship entitles him to an ally on demand. He offers a possibility and leaves Mitsuki with the responsibility of choosing. It is one of the best emotional choices of the early time-skip arc because it lets a powerful character demonstrate restraint without needing to lose the battle physically.

How Omnipotence might be resisted without a convenient cure

An important limit must be maintained here. Mitsuki's hesitation is not proof that Eida's power has been fully broken or that every character will recover exact memories through sincere affection. The chapter offers signs of tension inside an altered account of the world. It does not establish a universal antidote. A person can distrust a feeling, question a relationship and still retain the memory that makes that relationship seem correct. The difference is essential if the series wants the concept of Omnipotence to remain frightening rather than become a temporary obstacle overcome whenever the plot needs a reunion.

The chapter also suggests that resistance need not produce the same decision for every character. Sarada held to her understanding from the beginning; Amado reasons from technical evidence; Shikamaru infers a contradiction in his inherited narrative. Mitsuki confronts a more intimate failure: his devotion feels wrong despite its apparent logic. That variety is good characterization. It prevents the story from reducing its cast to a group of people waiting for one exposition scene. A mature resolution would allow Mitsuki to test his own beliefs rather than simply make him swap one unquestioning allegiance for another.

The hidden threat benefits from a quieter emotional chapter

Shikamaru's new unofficial coordination with Boruto and the continuing Divine Tree problem remain in the background. Chapter 7 is not primarily a new monster reveal, and it is stronger for knowing where its center lies. The future danger becomes more personal when the village's defenders cannot reliably distinguish allies from enemies. If Mitsuki continues hunting Boruto while the Divine Trees pursue their own targets, every misdirected resource creates another opportunity for harm. A single conversation cannot solve Konoha's political crisis, but the possibility of one fewer false enemy is strategically important.

The limitation is the story's appetite for momentum. Independent contemporary criticism celebrates Mitsuki's potential change while acknowledging how swiftly the combat resolves. That is a fair concern: even when an unequal fight serves a thematic purpose, it should not make a supporting character's preparation feel irrelevant. An extended exchange could have shown more of his abilities without sacrificing the emotional core. The chapter chooses clarity over breadth; readers are invited to remember what was said more than how many techniques were used. Whether that trade-off is worthwhile will depend on the space Mitsuki receives once the narrative's attention turns back to the larger threat.

Verdict: one of the arc's strongest statements about selfhood

The Whereabouts of the Sun is most convincing where it refuses the obvious resolution. Boruto could have told Mitsuki to believe him because they used to be friends. Instead, the argument reaches the harder conclusion that Mitsuki deserves a self beyond another person's light. As a response to Omnipotence, this is more interesting than a proof-of-identity puzzle. It asks what remains of a person when relationships and memories become unreliable, and whether independence can begin before certainty returns.

The criticism is that the imbalance in combat risks making Mitsuki an instrument for Boruto's characterization rather than an equal participant. Nevertheless, Mitsuki's questions and the hesitation documented by the official Naruto article make the exchange more than a victory lap. The chapter's lasting consequence is not a confirmed cure or a guaranteed alliance. It is a new way for Mitsuki to imagine loyalty as a choice. This assessment is original source-based critical analysis, grounded in Shueisha's release record, an official character retrospective and contemporaneous review coverage. It does not pretend to have verified every panel or assign a score without a firsthand reading. Chapter 8 should be judged partly on whether this insight changes how Mitsuki and Eida talk about love and agency.
