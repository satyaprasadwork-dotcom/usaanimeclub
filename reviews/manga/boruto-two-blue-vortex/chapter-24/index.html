Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 24 Critical Response: Kawaki's New Strength Buys Time, Not Forgiveness

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 24, Uzumaki Kawaki, examined: Kawaki's uncapped techniques, Jura's patience, Boruto's escape plan, the threat of Karma resonance and the brothers' unequal partnership.

Source-based commentary by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Kawaki's unlocked offense changes what the brothers can accomplish together, but this is not a victorious rematch or an emotional reconciliation. The chapter succeeds by defining survival as a planned retreat and keeping Jura dangerous.

The title is a challenge to the identity Kawaki has taken

Uzumaki Kawaki is a provocative title in a story where identities and families have been forcibly rearranged. The village believes Kawaki belongs in Naruto's household and that Boruto is the outsider who killed the Seventh Hokage. The reader knows that arrangement is a product of Omnipotence. The title can therefore be read as describing Kawaki's current public status and the part of his life he is desperate to protect, not an assurance that he has ethically earned every aspect of that status. Chapter 24 gives him a chance to defend somebody he has spent years threatening, but it does not cancel the decisions that caused the injustice.

The immediate action follows his entrance at the end of The Strong. With Amado's restrictions removed, Kawaki attacks Jura far more aggressively than before. Contemporary discussion identifies several familiar powers and an improvised use of a staff to convey energy toward the enemy. This is a significant shift for a character who has looked ineffective against the new Divine Trees despite holding a central position in the village. The chapter makes him useful again without necessarily making him right. That separation between capability and morality is essential to his role as Boruto's rival.

An offensive upgrade has a price that the action should remember

Chapter 18 explained that Amado configured Kawaki's altered body with an emphasis on defensive functions and regeneration. Adjusting those limits could increase his offensive output, but the change was not supposed to provide unlimited free strength. Chapter 24 begins testing the offensive side. Kawaki can exert pressure on Jura and force the villain to pay attention to attacks that would have been less threatening before. The abilities remain recognizably connected to the powers he acquired through Isshiki, so the improvement is less like an unexplained new transformation and more like a different way of using a complicated body.

The danger is that spectacle can obscure the drawback. A stronger attack is worth little if it leaves Kawaki vulnerable to an enemy who can respond with lethal precision. The fight does not require the reader to decide he is now stronger than Jura. In fact, contemporary criticism emphasizes that the villain remains far from defeated. Kawaki is buying a limited interval rather than demonstrating total superiority. That distinction is strategically credible and dramatically useful, because a character's value to a team need not depend on winning a duel without help.

The larger story should also retain the discomfort of the upgrade's origin. Amado can change Kawaki's body in ways that serve conflicting ambitions, and Kawaki previously resorted to threats to force him to cooperate. An improved combat performance is not proof the relationship has become less coercive. The person whose body was treated as a laboratory instrument is still trying to win control by forcing somebody else to change its settings. The unresolved ethical problem runs underneath the impressive new techniques.

Jura does not respond like a normal opponent in a rematch

Jura's behavior is one of the reasons the confrontation works. Contemporary accounts describe him receiving Kawaki's attacks with more curiosity than alarm, exploring what the sudden improvement means rather than immediately revealing that he is at his limit. The enemy is intelligent enough to interpret the change in power and to recognize how human emotions motivate extraordinary efforts. His composure prevents Kawaki's entrance from invalidating several chapters of threat-building. The opponent who could counter Uzuhiko and overwhelm Boruto should not become harmless because another fighter has arrived with a different configuration.

This places a new demand on Boruto and Kawaki. They cannot treat the encounter as an opportunity to resolve their private rivalry with Jura serving as an audience. The villain does not share their assumptions, and he can exploit any moment of divided attention. His interest in human affection has grown through Matsuri, Himawari and Sarada; Kawaki's desire to protect Naruto supplies another example. Jura increasingly sees the relationship between emotion and unexpected bursts of strength as a problem worth studying. The idea is intriguing, though the chapter should not be taken as proof that love literally increases every ninja's chakra under an established scientific rule.

A reasonable criticism is that Jura's curiosity may become a convenient explanation for why he does not end the conflict when he appears capable of doing so. An antagonist who is always experimenting can be allowed to postpone an obvious win almost indefinitely. The writing has to give him practical reasons for the delay, not rely exclusively on his desire to watch what happens. Here the heroes' plan provides some of that structure: they need only a small window, not a formal victory.

The real plan is evacuation, not a fantasy of winning two against one

The chapter's most useful revelation concerns the goal behind Kawaki's intervention. The brothers do not expect to defeat Jura in this state. Boruto has been trying to identify a marker that will let him teleport to safety, but he needs enough time and mental space to focus on it. Contemporary analysis describes a tracker in Konohamaru's possession providing the destination that can make a retreat possible. Kawaki's job is to occupy Jura long enough for Boruto to complete that process. This reframes the battle as a coordinated operation rather than an impulsive attack based on pride.

It is a better expression of tactical intelligence than assigning every powerful character a separate turn in the arena. Boruto may possess more refined techniques and broader knowledge of possible futures, while Kawaki brings direct offense that can interrupt Jura's pursuit. Neither advantage is sufficient by itself. The objective depends on timing, communication and the willingness to stop fighting when continuing would put everyone at risk. In the earliest chapters of Two Blue Vortex, Boruto seemed capable of arriving whenever a teammate needed a rescue. Here even escape is something people must create for him.

The plan also fits the story's theme of free will. Koji had viewed Boruto's prospects as bleak before the brothers' earlier conversation shifted the possibilities. The current outcome depends on Kawaki making a choice that is not simply programmed by the past or dictated by Omnipotence. He has not become a trustworthy partner, but he is capable of contributing to an immediate good outcome. That is a more interesting basis for cooperation than a sudden speech in which the two forgive everything.

Karma resonance threatens the fragile partnership

The confrontation acquires another source of danger when Kawaki's Karma and Boruto's Karma begin to interact. Published commentary describes Boruto recognizing the resonance and deliberately attempting to keep Momoshiki from gaining an opportunity to interfere. This is a strong reminder that the protagonist's own body cannot be treated as an uncomplicated instrument. His immense training and apparent composure do not abolish the threat that has been part of him since the original Boruto series. Standing near Kawaki creates a danger independent of Jura's attacks.

That matters because the very ally needed to create an escape could undermine Boruto's control if the resonance develops. The story has not yet established that Momoshiki will take over in this encounter, and critics should not present fan predictions as events that actually occur. The immediate consequence is tension and a reason Boruto must act quickly. The long-term consequence is structural: the brothers may be forced to cooperate in crises even though their powers and bodies create risks when brought together.

Kawaki's hostility toward the Ōtsutsuki presence remains connected to a genuine danger. That does not make his willingness to kill Boruto acceptable. A story capable of holding both facts at once can make their rivalry more substantial than a misunderstanding with supernatural causes. They have conflicting methods of protecting Naruto and conflicting beliefs about whose life can be sacrificed. A temporary fight on the same side is not an answer to those conflicts.

Jura's speculation about Naruto makes the victory smaller

By the end of the encounter, Jura appears to infer that Naruto is not dead. The possibility changes the nature of the threat to Konoha. Its defenders are attempting to preserve a secret about the former Hokage while living inside a false collective memory. An enemy who knows the official history is unreliable can start asking different questions about where Naruto is being kept and how to reach him. This creates a new vulnerability even if Boruto and Kawaki escape the immediate fight. The enemy gains knowledge while the heroes preserve their lives.

That is an effective exchange. Retreat should feel like success when survival is the objective, but it does not erase the cost of showing an unusually observant antagonist what the protagonists fear. The story is strongest when its villains learn. Jura has gradually moved from curiosity about ordinary human behavior to a more precise understanding of the people whose relationships influence the current conflict. His deductions could have consequences far beyond a single combat scene.

Verdict: cooperation is possible long before trust is restored

Uzumaki Kawaki succeeds by giving Kawaki a meaningful role without declaring that his power upgrade has solved every problem. His attacks occupy Jura, Boruto concentrates on a planned retreat, and the team escapes a fight it never had a credible reason to expect to win. The chapter also keeps the danger of Karma resonance visible and allows Jura to remain intelligent and threatening. These decisions make the battle more than a comeback spectacle.

The weaknesses are that the offensive improvement receives a dramatic showcase before its full long-term cost is clear, while Jura's willingness to keep watching could eventually feel too convenient. The brothers' cooperation is also necessarily thin: sharing a tactical objective does not undo the false identity, Naruto's imprisonment or Kawaki's earlier violence. That incompleteness is a strength if the story remembers it.
