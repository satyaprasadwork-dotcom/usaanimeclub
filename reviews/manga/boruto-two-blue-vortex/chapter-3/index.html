Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 3 Critical Response: Uzuhiko Changes the Fight, but Boruto's Real Advantage Is Patience

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 3, Uzuhiko, examined: the planet-linked Rasengan, Code's defeat, Kawaki's intervention, the hidden toad and the escalating Ten-Tails threat.

Source-based commentary by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Uzuhiko is memorable because its lingering effect turns Code's confidence into vulnerability. More importantly, Boruto's decision not to finish the fight gives the chapter strategic tension that a straightforward power demonstration would lack.

A technique that punishes confidence rather than just flesh

The promised reveal from Chapter 2 becomes the centerpiece of Chapter 3. Published reports describe Code attempting to understand Rasengan Uzuhiko as Boruto approaches him. He expects a recognizable variation on a familiar attack and tries to answer it through ordinary combat tactics. Instead, the technique uses the planet's rotation and produces a lasting destabilizing effect. The official Naruto character retrospective confirms the central idea: Uzuhiko is a new Rasengan drawing on the planet's motion, and Code is forced to retreat. That official explanation matters because the temptation is to convert the manga's evocative description into invented physical rules. The chapter does not require readers to mistake a supernatural ninjutsu principle for a real scientific claim.

What is distinctive is the kind of defeat the move creates. A conventional Rasengan leaves a clear image of impact; Uzuhiko makes Code's own sense of orientation unreliable. An opponent who normally imagines danger as something coming from outside is suddenly compromised by an ongoing disturbance he cannot simply dodge. This is a compelling power concept because it changes the psychology of the encounter. Boruto does not merely look faster or hit harder. The villain loses the dependable relationship between his body and the battlefield. The thematic connection to the series title is attractive, though it is interpretation rather than an explicit statement of authorial intention: the world itself now seems to turn against a man who has tried to reshape it.

The threat to Code is also an offer

Criticism published when the chapter appeared highlights how often Boruto gives Code an opportunity to cooperate. Boruto wants access to the Ten-Tails, not an immediate victory photograph. He is willing to stop the worsening effects of Uzuhiko if Code takes him where he needs to go. That negotiation creates the chapter's real suspense. The reader has seen enough of Boruto's ability to suspect he can win this exchange; the uncertainty shifts toward whether an arrogant adversary can make a rational decision under pressure. Code's resistance shows an inability to distinguish a personal humiliation from a danger larger than himself.

There is a credible weakness in this arrangement. Once Boruto is established as capable of overwhelming Code, keeping Code active requires either a convincing escape or a reason for the hero to hold back. The chapter offers the second explanation by giving Boruto a concrete strategic purpose. Whether that explanation stays persuasive depends on subsequent consequences. For now, it is more satisfying than a simple escalation in damage. An older Boruto who understands the necessity of using an enemy as a lead has genuinely changed, even if the manga has not yet shown us every step of the journey that made him this calculating.

Kawaki's intervention turns a nearly won duel into a chase

The fight does not resolve in a controlled laboratory of two characters. Kawaki arrives with others from Konoha, and the intervention disrupts Boruto's attempt to force the location of the Ten-Tails out of Code. Code uses the opening and his Claw Grime network to escape. This development is easy to read as a frustrating interruption. Boruto has just demonstrated a fascinating technique and appears to have his opponent exactly where he wants him; then the situation changes before a clean answer can arrive. But that frustration reflects the central social predicament of Two Blue Vortex. The people positioned to stop the same threat do not agree about who deserves trust.

Kawaki's presence has an additional edge because he is not an ordinary skeptical village guard. He knows much more about the identity exchange than Konoha does, and his opposition to Boruto is bound up with the secret he protects. The chapter therefore demonstrates that power alone cannot resolve the principal conflict. Even a terrifying Rasengan cannot persuade the village's official defenders to cooperate with the person they recognize as a traitor. Code's flight is a practical consequence of this division, not merely another contrived way to keep a villain available for later chapters.

A small toad makes the final turn more interesting

Published accounts of the ending describe Boruto having placed a toad on Code, allowing him to track the retreat toward the hidden Ten-Tails. It is a satisfyingly modest piece of strategy after the conceptual excess of Uzuhiko. The enormous planetary attack is not actually the whole solution; an unassuming surveillance measure becomes the means of pursuing the larger objective. That combination also strengthens the connection between Boruto's new persona and the older ninja traditions around him. The story's cleverness is not only in discovering a stronger technique but in making an opponent's probable escape part of the plan.

The resulting image of the Ten-Tails diminished or altered at Code's hideout introduces a new uncertainty. Boruto's urgency has not been explained away by his ability to beat one human opponent. Something is changing beyond the village's immediate view, and Code may not understand the system he thinks he commands. This is a more promising direction than a prolonged rematch, because it makes the antagonist's arrogance dangerous to the entire setting. The chapter closes with the feeling that a decisive individual victory may be strategically irrelevant if the underlying transformation has already begun.

The criticism: a remarkable power, an unresolved ceiling

Chapter 3 is strong at linking spectacle to a tactical objective and at letting Boruto demonstrate maturity through restraint. It is less convincing as a shared ensemble story. Sarada's moral position and Himawari's doubts from the first two chapters recede while the narrative concentrates on Boruto's new technique. That focus is understandable for the unveiling of Uzuhiko, but it also feeds a familiar concern: the title character may become so capable that everyone else is left to watch or obstruct him. A monthly release can magnify that imbalance, since an entire installment centered on one confrontation occupies a large portion of readers' experience of the arc.

The best response is not to demand that every fight be evenly balanced. Instead, the series must make power reveal character and create difficult decisions. Uzuhiko passes that test at first because Boruto needs information that brute force cannot guarantee, and because Kawaki's interference denies him the tidy outcome he deserves on raw ability. Its durability as a dramatic device is still an open question. This critical response relies on the official publisher, Naruto's official commentary and contemporary written reactions; it does not assign an unearned firsthand art or action score. The next chapter needs to show that the threat Boruto has pursued is larger and stranger than Code himself.
