Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 26 Review: Inojin's Loyalty Becomes Koji's Next Weapon Against Mamushi

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 26, Those Like-Minded, analyzed: Inojin and Himawari, Koji's recruitment, Shikamaru's investigation, Mamushi's intentions toward Eida, and Daemon confronting Sarada and Sumire.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Those Like-Minded turns friendship into an uncomfortable strategic resource. Koji can identify who will risk everything for Himawari, but his willingness to recruit that person by force raises the same questions about agency that surround his future visions.

A small gift reveals an enormous pressure on Inojin

Those Like-Minded begins with Inojin bringing honey to Himawari, an ordinary gesture between people whose relationship has quietly changed. The contrast with the preceding arc matters. Himawari nearly lost him during Jura's attack, and her sudden connection with Kurama offered impressive power without immediately teaching her how to use it safely. Inojin has already learned what being near a Divine Tree can cost. Contemporary coverage describes Kashin Koji approaching him through a toad after that visit, recognizing a personal attachment that could make the young ninja exceptionally determined to protect her. The manga turns a gentle social moment into the first step of another dangerous assignment.

The scene is more interesting than an uncomplicated declaration that love makes a person stronger. Inojin's feelings are his own, but Koji treats those feelings as evidence that the boy will be willing to accept risk. That is a leap with real moral implications. Being fond of somebody is not blanket consent to being recruited, transported or ordered into a plan built around disasters only one strategist has seen. Inojin's abilities may prove essential, and his desire to keep Himawari alive is understandable. The episode nevertheless encourages a question that the previous few chapters have been raising: when does an adviser stop protecting people and begin arranging their lives as if they were pieces on a board?

It also restores Inojin to the foreground. He was almost killed and recovered with surprising speed, but those earlier events risked functioning mainly as fuel for Himawari's awakening. A mission that depends on his own skills can help rebalance the narrative. The better outcome is not simply making him a stronger accessory to another character. It is allowing him to decide what help he can responsibly provide and to challenge the person who insists his future has already been calculated.

Koji's recruitment strategy is effective because it is unsettling

Contemporary criticism describes Inojin being brought to Koji's hidden location and introduced to the larger stakes surrounding the sentient Divine Trees. Koji has previously relied on Boruto, Shikamaru and a network of small toads to communicate information that the village's official security system cannot safely receive. His interest in a Yamanaka makes tactical sense. Inojin inherits a family tradition centered on communication and mental techniques, precisely the kind of expertise needed when an enemy can evade normal detection or create several bodies. It is good ensemble plotting to place a specialist in a job that a stronger punch cannot perform.

But Koji's method risks turning specialization into exploitation. The boy does not begin with a complete account of the possible futures or the freedom to evaluate the plan before being drawn into it. Koji controls the location, the timing and much of the information. He can sincerely wish to prevent catastrophe and still impose costs on somebody who has not chosen to become a member of his secret organization. That contradiction is important because the manga has sometimes treated Prescience as an explanation that automatically makes Koji's decisions sensible. Foresight gives him reasons, not an exemption from scrutiny.

Boruto's reaction adds needed friction. He is protective of his younger sister and impatient with detours that could leave her in danger. Yet his previous refusal to accept a predicted sacrifice of Yodo established that he does not entirely share Koji's calculus. The two can cooperate while disagreeing about the acceptable price of an outcome. Inojin's arrival gives that argument a new participant whose own life may be spent according to a vision he never requested to see.

Shikamaru faces an investigation that cannot be won by fighting

At the same time, the apparent recovery from the Sand Village mission has not made Konoha's political situation any safer. The village elders are suspicious of the circumstances surrounding Boruto's escape and of the Hokage's willingness to rely on information that appears to come from an enemy. A Fire Country official, Kobu, arrives to investigate possible help provided to the fugitive. Konohamaru's recent actions make him an obvious person to question, and the inquiry threatens to reach Mitsuki, Ino and others who have cooperated in small ways. This is a welcome continuation of Chapter 14's institutional dilemma rather than a new conflict invented for the convenience of this volume.

Shikamaru's difficulty is that the truth cannot simply be entered into an official report. Omnipotence has changed the remembered history of Boruto and Kawaki, and even sensible doubts can be destabilized by the effect on memory. A leader who says he has trusted Boruto could lose the authority required to protect the village against the Divine Trees. A leader who abandons the secret arrangement could leave Konoha unaware of an approaching catastrophe. He is trying to govern under an epistemic failure: the official evidence base is corrupted, but acknowledging that failure may make effective government impossible.

Unlike a conventional villain, Kobu does not need to possess extraordinary combat abilities to be dangerous. The power to summon, question and expose the people coordinating a secret defense can be enough. The risk is that the story portrays every skeptical official as incompetent or malicious to make Shikamaru look clever. There is a legitimate public-interest question about why Konoha's leadership has been secretly collaborating with an accused traitor. The stronger criticism targets a system that cannot investigate its own assumptions, not the mere existence of an investigation.

Jura's curiosity and Mamushi's appetite are different dangers

At the Divine Trees' location, Jura continues taking interest in human emotions and the inexplicable impact they have had on him. Contemporary commentary notes another reaction to a story about love, while Hidari remains curious about what such responses mean. Mamushi's purpose is far less romantic. He is intent on reaching Eida, whose extraordinary power he regards as a prize to be consumed or controlled. The distinction is vital. The Shinju are not a single personality repeated in different bodies. Their shared origins do not erase different temperaments and individual objectives.

Mamushi's interest in Eida is especially threatening because her existing powers have already deprived other people of genuine choice. Her charm alters how those around her behave, and Omnipotence changed an entire village's relationship to reality. Yet she is also a person who wants friendships that are not manufactured by those abilities. Mamushi approaches that very vulnerability as something to conquer. The chapter is disturbing because domination is being directed toward somebody who is herself trapped by mechanisms of involuntary influence. Treating the danger only as a joke about desire would erase what makes the encounter frightening.

The tension also gives Daemon a clear protective role. His extraordinary power is less important here than the intensity with which he responds to potential threats to his sister. But there is an obvious risk that his protective instinct will reproduce another form of coercion, particularly when he decides that anybody who might disappoint Eida is a danger. The chapter sets up that problem without resolving it.

Sarada and Sumire's immunity becomes an immediate liability

Eida realizes that Sarada and Sumire have been able to speak about their feelings for Boruto in a way people affected by her charm typically cannot. The conversation gives Eida reason to suspect that their apparent friendship has not been produced by the usual compulsion. Daemon reacts by grabbing both young women by their throats, a cliffhanger that makes the revelation dangerous rather than merely intriguing. The scene has been coming for some time. Their immunity has made the pair valuable as genuine friends, but it also leaves them vulnerable to being viewed as people who might act independently of Eida's interests.

An important distinction should remain in any careful explanation of this plot. Being unaffected by a charm-like attraction and retaining memories under Omnipotence are related narrative questions, but this installment does not settle every detail of why those exceptions occur. The chapter places the unusual status under examination; it does not prove an all-purpose genetic or romantic theory of immunity. Readers who want a definite mechanism are justified in expecting a later explanation. Confident guesses about Ōtsutsuki ancestry or hidden abilities would go beyond the evidence presented at this point.

Sarada and Sumire's conduct is also open to criticism. They have spent years keeping their exceptional status concealed, and a conversation that openly reveals their independence is a tactical lapse. But the lapse should not reduce them to foolish background characters. Eida has learned from their friendship and is capable of drawing conclusions from small emotional contradictions. Their vulnerability exposes how difficult it is to sustain a private identity in the presence of somebody who can observe so much of the world.

Verdict: an interlude about the cost of being understood

Those Like-Minded moves less quickly than the previous battles, but its threads reinforce one another. Koji understands Inojin's attachment well enough to recruit him. Konoha's elders understand enough about Shikamaru's secrecy to make his position precarious. Mamushi sees Eida as something he can possess, while Eida finally recognizes that two people close to her may be immune to her influence. The chapter's connective tissue is knowledge used to direct other people's choices. That makes the title more unsettling than a simple expression of friendship.

The weaknesses are meaningful. The extended conversations slow momentum, Koji's willingness to control information can feel like an excuse for moving people into position, and the cliffhanger risks making Sarada and Sumire appear helpless immediately after their important character development. Yet the story has identified a more intriguing enemy than a larger battle score: the loss of autonomy when others decide they understand what matters to you.
