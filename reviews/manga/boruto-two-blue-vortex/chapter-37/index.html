Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 37 Review: Sarada's Earring Makes Trust More Useful Than Koji's Foresight

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 37, Trust, reviewed: Sarada's marker improvisation after Code removes Boruto's seals, Momoshiki's possible takeover, Koji cutting Boruto off, Hidari's competing claim, Jura's cat books and Kobu's evidence against Shikamaru.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Chapter 37 works because its central choice is neither absolute obedience to Koji nor reckless optimism. Sarada adapts the failed marker plan herself, creating a way for Boruto to find her while a possible future warns that saving her may expose him to Momoshiki.

The official English title is a useful guide to the plot

Trust is the official English title of Chapter 37, despite early translations that circulated with a longer wording equivalent to believing in a particular person. The distinction is worth explaining because the people in this chapter are not simply asked to believe something will happen. They must decide whether to act when no reliable prediction guarantees their safety. Sarada has willingly been carried away by Code. Boruto's original teleportation markers have been removed. Koji has received a new vision that suggests the rescue itself could contribute to Momoshiki's takeover. Nearly every participant has good reasons to distrust the situation.

The chapter does not make trust into a magical solution to incomplete information. It shows several different forms. Sarada trusts that Boruto will understand her clue, Boruto trusts that she is still capable of helping herself, Koji selectively trusts his allies with warnings he refuses to share with Boruto, and Shikamaru asks an ordinary bookseller to trust him with a task involving Jura. Even Code trusts his own plan too much. The same word therefore describes informed loyalty, untested assumptions and dangerous overconfidence. Its value depends on whether the person extending trust can recognize when the evidence has changed.

A careful review should also resist turning the chapter into an announcement that Sarada and Boruto have become a couple. Their closeness is important to the action, but the narrative consequence is a coordinated response to an abduction. The plot does not need to settle their future romantic relationship to make the moment meaningful. Two shinobi who know each other's methods can work together even while frightened and separated.

The flashback establishes why a marker is more important than strength

An additional conversation between Boruto and Sarada explains the purpose of letting Code abduct her. The Divine Trees' hideout cannot be reached by simply choosing it as a destination. Boruto needs a marking object that connects him with the place before he can use his version of Flying Thunder God to enter it. Code's Claw Marks offer a route for transporting Sarada, but their existence is not an automatic invitation for Boruto to follow. The plan therefore depends on her carrying his marker into a place where the enemies do not know they have been given a future access point.

That is a sound premise for the story's particular collection of techniques. Boruto has gained significant mobility since the time skip, but earlier chapters established that a destination must be identified and the technique requires concentration. His powers are dramatically more interesting when planning affects the outcome. A difficult infiltration can demand the cooperation of people other than the protagonist. Sarada is not asked merely to remain still until he arrives; she is supposed to transport the practical condition that makes his arrival possible.

Code's discovery of those objects in the preceding chapter is the failure the new installment has to address. It would be frustrating if the story ignored the objects' removal or quietly invented a new marker already inside the hideout. The chapter is stronger because the loss is allowed to matter. Boruto is understandably alarmed, and his usual ability to move between distant locations is insufficient until he has another trace to target.

Koji's decision to exclude Boruto is tactically defensible and ethically unstable

When Boruto demands an update, Koji removes him from the communication network linking the strategist with other allies. According to contemporary discussion, the immediate reason is Momoshiki: information received by Boruto may also reach the Ōtsutsuki presence sharing his body. Koji has seen a possible future in which Momoshiki regains control and the consequences are disastrous. Keeping Boruto unaware of the most sensitive details could deny Momoshiki an advantage. This is a genuine tactical problem, not a trivial argument between friends.

But the decision also repeats Koji's familiar pattern of choosing who may know things about their own life. Boruto is the person expected to face the danger, and he is being denied the information needed to understand it. Koji may have compelling reasons, but those reasons do not make the relationship equal. Earlier chapters established that the mere act of sharing a forecast could change it, making secrecy useful in some circumstances. Chapter 37 raises a sharper objection: what happens when the person being protected cannot tell the difference between a well-grounded safeguard and a manipulation designed to make him act in a particular way?

The strongest version of the scene allows both concerns to remain real. Sharing a precise account of Momoshiki's potential takeover might accelerate the disaster if Momoshiki hears every word. Refusing to explain anything risks turning Boruto into an instrument of a plan whose consequences he cannot consent to. The chapter does not produce a tidy compromise. It lets the tension remain a moral burden on an adviser who has repeatedly used superior information to direct other people.

The takeover warning is possible, not an event that has already happened

Koji's latest vision introduces Momoshiki as a renewed immediate threat. His report suggests that saving Sarada is among the factors that might lead to Boruto losing control. That matters because Chapter 29 established an agreement under which Momoshiki would assist Boruto in exchange for the possibility of eventually taking the body. The bargain already created a dangerous future obligation, and now Koji sees a route toward that danger arriving sooner and under less controlled circumstances than expected.

Readers should distinguish the content of a warning from a confirmed outcome. Chapter 37 does not establish that Momoshiki has permanently taken over Boruto, nor that the rescue must be abandoned to keep him contained. The forecasts depend on combinations of decisions and events that may still change. Koji's warning is serious evidence of risk but not proof that all alternatives have disappeared. Previous chapters have demonstrated that an unanticipated act by another person can change which futures remain likely.

Momoshiki's interest in Boruto's emotional condition is particularly unsettling. The alien can observe the panic Boruto struggles to hide, recognizing how deeply he cares about Sarada. That makes the rescue situation a potential means of influencing his host even if Momoshiki does not yet take control. The manga is careful to show a person seeking to protect somebody while living with an adversary who regards every strong feeling as a possible opening. The emotional relationship and the supernatural threat are linked without being the same thing.

Sarada creates her own way for Boruto to find her

Code's removal of the prepared objects does not leave Sarada without agency. As the chapter revisits the moments of her abduction, she uses her Mangekyō ability to create an opportunity and tears out an earring that carries her chakra, leaving a trace Boruto might detect. The detail is effective because it turns an ordinary personal item into an improvised rescue signal without requiring an unrelated new ability. Sarada recognizes the purpose of the original plan and tries to reproduce its essential condition with something Code did not take.

The scene is also a rebuke to the recurring idea that she exists primarily to be rescued. Sarada has agreed to a dangerous operation for reasons that include her desire to save Sasuke. When that operation fails, she identifies a way to help from inside the situation. The act is not enough to guarantee safety, and it does not erase the problem of agreeing to be a hostage in the first place. It does show the kind of judgment that her ambition to become Hokage requires: protecting the larger objective under pressure while anticipating what an ally will understand.

Eida's observation adds a complicated layer. The Senrigan allows her to recognize Sarada's intention, but it is still surveillance. The fact that Eida may appreciate the trust between two friends does not mean she has an unrestricted right to monitor them. The manga often presents her remarkable access to information as both a tactical service and an obstacle to ordinary relationships. The earring scene is strongest when it centers Sarada's choice rather than turning her private feelings into another spectacle for an observer.

Code's hostage strategy collides with Hidari's independent objective

Code arrives in the Divine Trees' hideout expecting to use Sarada as bargaining leverage against Boruto. The plan is immediately undermined by Hidari, whose purpose is connected to Sarada and to his origin through Sasuke. Code regards the prisoner as his tool for drawing out the person he hates. Hidari regards her as the target of a different need. Neither has reason to recognize the other's claim as decisive, and neither treats Sarada's wishes as a meaningful boundary. Their conflict transforms a supposedly controlled hostage situation into an unstable confrontation among people whose desired outcomes cannot all be achieved.

That conflict is a practical advantage for Sarada, but a dangerous one. An enemy arguing with another enemy can create room to act; the same disagreement can make both less predictable. She considers how the opposing interests might be exploited, much as she previously cooperated with the Sand team to counter Ryū. However, her recent problems with the stamina cost of Ohirume make it especially important that she does not try to solve every part of the confrontation through repeated large-scale techniques.

The chapter is careful not to finish the fight. Code and Hidari are both present, and Sarada has been forced to improvise, but the action that follows belongs in the next installment. Criticism that casually attributes the full three-way battle or Boruto's eventual strike to Chapter 37 obscures the pacing of this particular turning point. The achievement here is making the danger intelligible: Code brought a person another enemy wants, then discovered that the enemy has no intention of cooperating with his hostage plan.

Jura's cat books make the village's scariest delay absurdly human

While the rescue problem develops, Jura remains in a Konoha bookshop searching for books about cats to share with Hidari. The scene is peculiar in the best way. A creature capable of devastating the village is pleased by the prospect of learning what interests his companion. Earlier chapters revealed his sadness at the thought of losing Hidari, and this visit provides a modest behavioral expression of the attachment. He can be a terrifying existential threat and also become absorbed in an ordinary act of choosing a gift.

Shikamaru identifies the opportunity. If Jura can be kept occupied in the shop, he will be less likely to interfere while Boruto attempts to reach the hideout. Shikamaru therefore asks the shopkeeper, Furuta, to continue entertaining the customer despite the enormous stakes. This is an amusing strategy with an uncomfortable price. A civilian is being made responsible for preventing a fight that professional shinobi cannot safely win. The humor works because the commander knows exactly how absurd his request sounds. The ethical problem remains because the person being asked to help is not protected by the authority that imposes the responsibility.

Jura's emerging affection also complicates his earlier theory that emotional bonds make humans irrational. He has begun to act on feelings himself, but the story has not yet established that doing so makes him kinder to outsiders. The bookstore scene offers behavioral change without redemption, a distinction worth preserving.

Kobu threatens Shikamaru at the worst possible moment

Shikamaru's effort to manage Jura and the rescue is interrupted by news that Kobu has new evidence concerning Boruto's escape. The investigator believes the acting Hokage helped someone officially regarded as a traitor. The case has moved beyond vague suspicion toward a claim that a witness and physical details can connect the escape to the leadership. This is not merely a personal inconvenience for Shikamaru. If he loses the authority needed to coordinate the defense, the village may be forced to face Jura and the remaining Divine Trees without its most informed organizer.

The investigation should not be portrayed as inherently ridiculous. Under the history imposed by Omnipotence, government officials have reason to examine a possible conspiracy around the person blamed for Naruto's death. Their evidence may be consistent with a false story, but the duty to question official misconduct remains legitimate. The more disturbing political possibility is that the investigation is being used to pursue a broader agenda against the shinobi system. Chapter 37 raises the pressure but does not yet install a new Hokage or resolve what the investigating faction plans to do after Shikamaru is removed.

The timing makes the conflict especially sharp. Shikamaru needs to keep Jura occupied for a brief window, and Kobu demands the attention of the same official at the same time. The situation cannot be solved through an immediate combat victory. It requires choices about what can be postponed, which information can be trusted and who will bear the consequences of an interrupted command.

Verdict: trust is an action, not an assurance

Trust is one of the more coherent transitional chapters in this stretch of Two Blue Vortex. The earring gives Sarada a meaningful role in correcting the failed operation. Koji's secrecy tests whether an alliance built on privileged knowledge can withstand the possibility that the person most at risk is being kept uninformed. Code's attempt to exploit Sarada crashes into Hidari's independent objective. Jura's affection for Hidari creates a rare noncombat method of delaying a deadly enemy, while Kobu's pressure brings the institutional crisis into the same narrow window of time.

The drawbacks are considerable: Sarada's voluntary kidnapping is an inherently dangerous premise, the political arc can feel slow beside the rescue, and Koji's warnings create a growing risk of explaining each complication before the reader experiences it. But this chapter gives a better answer than simply asking the protagonist to fight harder. Sarada acts because she trusts Boruto to recognize a signal. Boruto must decide what to do without knowing everything. The consequences remain unresolved, as they should.
