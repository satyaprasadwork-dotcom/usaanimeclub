Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 36 Review: Amado Calls It Parental Love, but Koji Never Agreed to Be Changed

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 36, Parental Love, analyzed: Amado alters Koji's Prescience, his plan to revive Akebi through Kawaki's Karma, the failure of Boruto and Sarada's marker strategy, and Code abducting Sarada.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Chapter 36 works because its central conflict is consent, not just a new prediction. Amado can improve Koji's ability but has no right to modify his mind. His parental explanation is especially unsettling alongside the desperate plan to restore Akebi, while Sarada's abduction makes the limits of foresight painfully immediate.

The frightening part of the kidnapping happened before Koji woke up

Parental Love takes the abduction of Kashin Koji, introduced in the preceding chapter, and reveals what the scientist actually wanted. Amado had persuaded Kawaki to help him obtain access to Koji, presenting the operation as compatible with the village's effort to defeat the sentient Divine Trees. Kawaki's own reasons for cooperating are pragmatic rather than affectionate: he wants sufficient power to eliminate the threats to Naruto. But their overlapping short-term objectives hide a severe difference in how they regard other people. Kawaki sees Koji as potentially useful intelligence. Amado sees a creation whose body and mind he can alter to increase the usefulness of that intelligence.

Contemporary accounts explain that Amado modifies Koji's brainwaves while he is incapacitated, attempting to make Prescience activate more often. The target is a genuine weakness. Koji can observe possible futures, but he cannot summon fresh updates whenever the plans change. His earlier warnings became less reliable once Boruto, Inojin and the defenders acted on information about the future and changed the conditions that produced it. A more frequent view of possibilities could help protect the village. That benefit does not settle whether Amado had the right to perform an invasive procedure without permission.

The scene is unsettling precisely because the technique may work. Fiction often presents an unethical experiment as obviously wrong by making it fail immediately. Here the potential result is beneficial enough that both the characters and readers may be tempted to excuse the method. The chapter asks whether greater strategic accuracy is worth letting a scientist decide that a person can be modified whenever the outcome seems desirable. It is a question the story should not answer merely by showing Koji receiving a useful vision later.

Amado's definition of parenthood is a claim of ownership

Amado calls his act an expression of parental feeling because he created Koji. The framing makes Chapter 35's interest in newly discovered attachment considerably darker. Jura has begun to experience sadness at the thought of losing Hidari, a feeling that might lead him to protect another being whose existence he values. Amado is already familiar with grief and love, yet uses the language of fatherhood to justify control. He acts as though originating a life gives him continuing authority to alter that life against its wishes. That is not the same claim as caring for the person's welfare.

Koji's history makes the distinction sharper. He was constructed with a purpose, used in a fight Amado had calculated in advance and left facing lethal danger without full knowledge of the plan. A creator's willingness to dispose of a person for a strategic result does not become a demonstration of love when the creator later decides to upgrade the survivor's abilities. The chapter is effective when Koji recognizes the gap between Amado's pleasant explanation and the violence of the underlying act. An ability designed to forecast the consequences of coercion does not make the act less coercive.

Not every relationship in the sequel permits a straightforward moral comparison. Kawaki himself has experienced bodily exploitation and remains capable of exploiting others. Boruto considers giving Momoshiki control over his body to protect other people, creating a different form of ethically dangerous agreement. The specific problem here is lack of consent. Koji was not consulted about an alteration to his brain. Whether the procedure eventually saves lives, harms his health or produces new information cannot retroactively supply a choice that was denied to him.

Akebi is a daughter, not simply a restoration mechanism

Amado's broader motivation remains the possible return of Akebi. A prepared body resembling his daughter exists, and he believes that information embedded in Kawaki's Karma can be used to restore her identity through a method related to Ōtsutsuki resurrection. He asks Koji whether any observed future contains the moment he wants: Kawaki applying Karma to the clone so Akebi can live again. Koji cannot offer reassurance. Contemporary analyses report that he has not seen the desired outcome, while the futures in which Jura appears remain extremely dangerous for Kawaki and the village.

The discussion gives Amado more emotional dimension than a generic laboratory villain. Grief can explain why somebody devotes years to a project with little chance of success. It cannot make every action in that project's name permissible. Kawaki is a person who has been used as a vessel since childhood. The body prepared for Akebi raises questions of memory, identity and the ability of a restored person to choose her own life. Amado is acting on an image of the daughter he lost, not on instructions from someone who can presently consent. Those are serious ethical costs even before the practical question of whether Karma would operate as intended.

The chapter does not establish that Akebi's resurrection is impossible in every future. Koji has access only to particular possibilities, and this installment has just changed the conditions of his ability. A claim that none of the futures he has seen offers confirmation is narrower than a declaration that the plan can never succeed. The scientist's next decision will be interesting because he has been denied the single assurance he hoped to obtain from a person who can look ahead.

Prescience creates a dangerous temptation to control the person who sees

The chapter's central irony is that Koji's apparent advantage may make him vulnerable. Someone who can witness possible outcomes becomes valuable to every faction that wants to decide which outcome to pursue. Shikamaru needs warnings about threats to the village. Boruto needs advice about how to protect friends without triggering catastrophe. Amado wants confirmation about his daughter. Each request might seem reasonable in isolation, but the pressure becomes overwhelming when the owner of the ability is expected to answer without controlling what happens to his own mind.

An improved frequency of visions might also generate new strategic confusion. More information does not necessarily produce better decisions when every informed action changes the future being observed. Amado's intervention is capable of increasing the rate at which scenarios appear, but that does not prove the resulting data will be easier to interpret. It may reveal conflicting possibilities faster than the characters can evaluate them. This possibility is more grounded than assuming Amado has suddenly obtained a perfect recording of all the future's events. The chapter establishes an alteration and its intended purpose, not limitless prediction.

Koji's response matters because the audience has already watched him compromise other people's autonomy. He attempted to make Inojin abandon Kobu, and he has calculated losses among allies as though their willingness to take those losses were secondary. Now he faces a version of the same problem directed at himself. The parallel does not erase what Amado has done; it makes the story's treatment of agency more coherent.

A new vision does not make Sarada's abduction preventable

Once the confrontation with Amado concludes, Koji contacts Boruto with disturbing information. A possible future indicates that the planned operation involving Code and Sarada is no longer following the assumptions that made it attractive. The mission was to allow Code to carry Sarada into the Divine Trees' hideout with a teleportation marker, making the location accessible to Boruto for a later rescue or operation. It depended on Code behaving according to a plan he was never told. The difficulty is that Code is himself capable of observing a prisoner and removing suspicious objects.

Contemporary chapter reporting describes Code confronting Sarada, identifying Boruto's markers and stripping them away. She is abducted despite the preparation she and Boruto agreed upon. That is an especially credible kind of failure. The hero knows a useful technique and has a plan; the enemy does not have to be more powerful than him to defeat the plan. Code only needs to notice that the objects on Sarada may have a tactical function. A carefully arranged infiltration becomes a hostage situation when the underlying assumption about his behavior collapses.

The chapter earns its cliffhanger because Sarada made a choice with considerable risk. She agreed to act as bait in part to reach the place where Hidari remains connected to Sasuke's imprisonment. That willingness should not be mistaken for a lack of agency. Yet a reader can still question whether Boruto's secrecy about his negotiations with Momoshiki left her with enough information to evaluate the consequences. She consented to a particular plan, not necessarily to every danger that would follow if the plan failed.

A relationship can be built on trust without becoming safe

Sarada's loyalty to Boruto is increasingly being tested under conditions created by other people's powers. Omnipotence has made the village treat him as an enemy, and the need for secrecy has repeatedly separated the two friends. Sarada's decision to cooperate with a plan built around her own kidnapping illustrates extraordinary trust, but it also shows why trust must not replace practical safeguards. A dependable ally should be able to tell another person when an outcome is uncertain and when the plan carries risks they cannot fully predict.

Boruto's concern becomes more credible when he cannot immediately use Flying Thunder God to undo the abduction. The chapter has already established that his markers and preparation matter. Removing the objects is not a trivial obstacle that disappears because the protagonist wants to rescue somebody. It gives a new job to Sarada, who must survive inside an enemy location and potentially find an alternative means of communicating where she has been taken. The next installment can either reward that initiative or reduce her to an inert prize waiting for another arrival.

This is the chapter's best corrective to the idea that seeing the future provides security. Koji has more information but cannot command Code's behavior. Amado has new technology but cannot secure his daughter's return. Boruto has exceptional mobility but cannot reach a location when the needed marker is absent. Every powerful character is being forced to confront a different limit.

Verdict: useful exposition made uncomfortable by who controls it

Parental Love is conversation-heavy, and its principal accomplishments involve clarifying motives rather than resolving a battle. That can feel slow after the long Mamushi conflict. But the chapter's focus is unusually coherent. Amado treats Koji as something he may modify because he created him, seeks a prediction about the return of Akebi and inadvertently reinforces the story's argument that information does not equal control. The failure of Boruto and Sarada's marker strategy supplies an immediate example of how a calculated plan can collapse when another person makes an unexpected choice.

The weaknesses are the convenient pace of scientific breakthroughs and the danger that Sarada's kidnapping will serve mainly to motivate the men who intend to rescue or consume her. Those issues require the subsequent chapters to preserve her judgment. The strongest critical point remains that the scientist's language of love does not justify taking away the freedom of a person he created.
