Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 34 Review: Sakura Trusts Her Family When the World Insists It Is Wrong

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 34, Used to It, examined: Bug's Thorn Soul time limit, Code's interference, Sakura and Sarada's first substantial conversation after Omnipotence, the cost of Mangekyō, and Chocho reassessing Boruto.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

The chapter's strongest moment is quiet: Sakura supports Sarada without pretending her family's situation is painless. Against that compassionate scene, Bug's fate proves that a Thorn Soul Bulb offers only a brief and dangerous chance at rescue.

The Thorn Soul Bulb is a rescue tool with a devastating deadline

After the defeat of Mamushi, the chapter reveals a detail that fundamentally changes how readers should understand the rescue of people trapped inside Divine Trees. Boruto explains to Kawaki that a recovered Thorn Soul Bulb cannot simply be stored while the team waits for a safer opportunity. Contemporary accounts describe it deteriorating within a short period if it is not used either to restore the captive or in a process that produces another sentient tree. The consequence reaches beyond the loss of an object: the original person connected to the bulb is also at risk. A victory in battle therefore creates a second emergency rather than resolving everything automatically.

That rule is especially important for Sasuke. Readers have spent a long stretch of the sequel imagining a future confrontation with Hidari that ends with the restoration of Sarada's father. The new deadline means the fight itself cannot be planned independently of the rescue location. If a Thorn Soul Bulb is recovered too far from Sasuke's tree, the characters may lose their opportunity while attempting to reach him. Boruto's unusual mobility becomes strategically vital, but his restrictions still matter. The ability to teleport is not a universal answer when concentration, markers and hostile interference can consume the time available.

The detail also corrects an earlier temptation to treat these objects like prizes awarded after a boss battle. A living person's future is not safely secured merely because the enemy has been defeated. Careful planning and immediate action are required, and the characters need to know exactly whom they are trying to save. That makes the mechanics ethically relevant as well as suspenseful.

Code transforms the promise of Bug's recovery into a loss

Boruto attempts to use Mamushi's Thorn Soul Bulb to rescue Bug, whose confinement enabled the creation of the enemy. But Code has moved into position, and the encounter prevents Boruto from reaching the captive in time. The reported result is Bug's death. This is a striking choice because the previous arc offered examples of the Thorn Soul process succeeding. A reader might reasonably assume that once a bulb is recovered, restoration is a matter of finding the tree and performing an established step. Chapter 34 makes clear that even the correct method can fail when the characters are delayed.

Code's role is more than another demonstration of his anger toward Boruto. He has a personal connection to Bug, yet his fixation on conflict prevents the rescue of a man whose fate should matter to him. That is a meaningful expression of the difference between grievance and responsibility. Code can believe that revenge will solve his problems and still be incapable of acting in a way that protects the people caught up in them. His intervention has consequences more permanent than losing another fight. It changes the stakes of any future attempt to save Sasuke.

Some readers may find Code's repeated failures as a direct antagonist frustrating. A character can look less threatening each time a carefully announced ambush fails to secure his intended victory. But his failure to defeat Boruto does not make him harmless; this chapter shows he can cause irreversible damage by delaying the hero. That is a more credible danger than granting him an unexplained strength increase just so the next fight can be advertised as bigger.

Koji's alternate futures help without offering comfort

Koji has identified several possible routes involving Code. One includes Code's interest in using Sarada to lure Boruto into a confrontation with the remaining Divine Trees; another leaves open the faint possibility that Code may change his allegiance. That second outcome is treated as highly unlikely, not as an announced coming redemption. The distinction matters. The information gives Boruto reasons to prepare for specific threats, but it does not establish which outcome will happen. Koji has already admitted that predictions shift when people act on them and that his access to new visions is intermittent.

The chapter's use of this uncertainty is effective because a plan can fail even when the hero knows about the most obvious ambush. Boruto may approach Code expecting trouble and still be unable to save Bug before the bulb deteriorates. Foresight is no substitute for the capacity to execute a rescue under pressure. Readers should take care not to inflate the set of possible futures into a detailed script for the next arc. Koji's warnings are scenarios that influence decisions, not confirmed evidence that Code will soon kidnap Sarada or join the protagonists.

The critical weakness is that the story increasingly relies on Koji to supply the next obstacle. A long series of predicted encounters could make the world feel like a collection of future spoilers given to the hero in advance. Chapter 34 pushes against that risk through Bug's fate. The fact that Boruto anticipated interference does not stop the worst outcome, restoring the possibility of genuine loss.

Sakura finally receives a conversation worthy of her history

The most welcome sequence follows Sarada waking in the hospital and speaking with Sakura about the effects of Mangekyō Sharingan. The ability that won a battle against Ryū and helped protect civilians has begun to affect her eyesight, raising the possibility of eventual blindness if she continues using it recklessly. Sakura is uniquely positioned to understand the burden. She is a medical professional and a woman whose family has been repeatedly placed under suspicion because of its relationship to people the village calls traitors. She knows that her daughter is not simply seeking a more powerful technique; she is trying to defend a person everyone else insists deserves punishment.

Sakura's response avoids the easiest extremes. She does not dismiss the danger of eye damage, nor does she order Sarada to abandon her beliefs because the village disapproves. She recognizes that earlier members of the Uchiha family have paid serious prices for the paths they chose. She can warn her daughter about consequences while acknowledging the legitimacy of the values that motivate her. That is a more credible expression of parenting than either celebrating every injury as proof of courage or treating the existence of a risk as a reason to surrender responsibility.

The conversation gives Sakura an emotional role that was missing from several earlier arcs. Sasuke's departure to protect Boruto must have affected her life, especially because the village's memories make that decision appear indefensible. The manga should have explored her position sooner. Allowing her finally to speak makes the family's predicament feel lived-in rather than something that exists only to motivate Sarada's next attack.

The title belongs to Sakura's resilience, not indifference

Used to It sounds at first like an expression of resignation. Sakura has faced suspicion surrounding Sasuke before, and the world has repeatedly expected her to justify why she trusts him. Now the scrutiny extends to her daughter and the decision to defend Boruto. The chapter's emotional power comes from the difference between being accustomed to criticism and being unaffected by it. Sakura can maintain a calm face in front of Sarada, helping her feel supported, and still experience grief about how much the family has been asked to endure.

That distinction makes her more compelling than a character whose emotional strength is defined as never crying. A parent can be resolute while privately suffering. A medical leader can warn somebody about a dangerous technique and accept that refusing to use it may cause other losses. The responsibility is not to abolish pain. It is to avoid letting pain dictate every response. Sakura's position also honors the previous generation's history without claiming she alone can reverse Omnipotence or rescue Sasuke immediately.

The major weakness is the delay. The story has had many opportunities to examine how Sakura interpreted Sasuke's decision after the world's memories changed. Her absence from those discussions has made the family's predicament feel strangely isolated. This chapter offers a satisfying conversation but cannot erase the need for future scenes in which her judgment shapes events beyond providing comfort in a hospital room.

Chocho's changing view of Boruto is political as well as personal

Chocho begins to question the story she has been told about Boruto. She has fought beside him during the Mamushi emergency and seen actions that are difficult to reconcile with the idea that he is simply a hostile traitor. She also notices contradictions in the accepted account of Kawaki's relationship to Naruto. Those observations do not prove that Omnipotence has suddenly stopped working or that Chocho's memories have been restored. They do show that a person can encounter new evidence and change how she behaves even while living with a distorted personal history.

That is an encouraging development for the narrative's long-running memory problem. If everyone could only return to the truth when the spell was formally lifted, years of relationships would have no opportunity to matter. Chocho's willingness to reassess Boruto gives Sarada something she has desperately needed: a friend capable of taking the evidence of recent actions seriously. The chapter becomes more interesting when trust is understood as something people build through choices rather than simply something restored by a powerful jutsu.

At the same time, the story should not treat a few observations as an effortless cure for a cosmic-scale rewrite. Chocho may believe Boruto is trying to protect Konoha without accepting every claim about his parentage or the original events. That intermediate position is credible. Trust can begin with one witnessed act before it grows into agreement about a wider history.

Verdict: a recovery chapter about losses that cannot be reversed

Used to It is important for two contrasting reasons. The Thorn Soul rule creates urgency and proves through Bug's death that a rescue can fail even after the enemy has been defeated. Sakura's conversation with Sarada offers an emotional counterweight: some forms of harm cannot be prevented by having perfect information, but relationships can help people endure what they face. Chocho's shift also suggests that Omnipotence need not make every person incapable of reconsidering the world around them. These developments advance the story without requiring another giant fight.

The drawbacks are the late arrival of Sakura's perspective, the risk of repeating future-vision exposition and Code's increasingly uneven reputation as an antagonist. Yet the chapter puts an appropriate cost on its central promises. The ability to save trapped people depends on timing; the Mangekyō can protect others while hurting its user; and a family can stand by somebody without being spared the social consequences.
