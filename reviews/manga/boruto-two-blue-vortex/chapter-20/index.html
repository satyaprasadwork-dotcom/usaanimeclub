Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 20 Review: Sarada's Mangekyō Sharingan Is a Choice to Protect, Not a Contest of Power

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 20, Because of Love, critically examined: Sarada's Mangekyō Sharingan and gravitational effect, Yodo's offer, Ryū's Iron Sand control, Konohamaru's Giant Rasengans and Jura's response to Matsuri.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Because of Love gives Sarada a compelling turning point while refusing to make affection harmless. Konohamaru uses Matsuri's attachment as a weapon, Yodo offers herself for her friends and Sarada answers helplessness with a new ability whose rules are not yet fully established.

The chapter's title is both an explanation and a warning

Because of Love seems to promise a straightforward emotional resolution. These characters care about one another, so surely that care will give them the strength to win. The actual reported events resist anything so easy. Konohamaru exploits Matsuri's attachment to him, Yodo contemplates surrendering herself to save her teammates, and Sarada reaches for a dangerous power while the others are being controlled by Ryū. Affection creates courage, but it also creates pressure and openings for an enemy. The chapter is interesting because the same force driving its heroic choices can be used to manipulate someone else.

The framing grows naturally out of the previous installment's argument about love as a potential obstacle to rational action. Jura wants the Divine Trees to rise above such distractions, but Matsuri has become attached to Konohamaru without knowing how to live with the attachment. The human protagonists are equally capable of behaving irrationally. The difference is not that human love is automatically virtuous while the creatures' feelings are automatically evil. It is that the ninja must decide whether protecting someone can justify deception, self-sacrifice or violence. The story gives several answers in the same chapter and allows those answers to clash.

That is more valuable than using the title as a general slogan about friendship. Love is a reason, not a permission slip. If someone claims to act for a friend, the reader can still question the effect of the action on the friend and the people nearby. Chapter 20's most mature possibility is that it asks the cast to defend the people they care about without assuming their own feelings make every method acceptable.

Konohamaru's strategy works precisely because it feels wrong

Contemporary criticism describes Konohamaru talking Matsuri into taking his hands while she struggles between wanting him close and wanting to consume him. The scene pays off Shikamaru's earlier instruction to make use of that emotional conflict. Konohamaru responds with two Giant Rasengans, devastating much of Matsuri's body. The tactic is successful in the immediate sense: a fighter who could not defeat the creature by brute force creates a vulnerable moment by taking advantage of the trust she wants to place in him. He has finally acted rather than remaining trapped inside a conversation about Moegi.

Yet the achievement is uncomfortable, and it ought to be. Matsuri has only recently begun to experience attachment, and Konohamaru uses her confusion to damage her. She is a deadly opponent whose continued existence threatens Moegi and others, but she is also a newly conscious being drawn into a feeling she cannot decipher. The narrative risks making manipulation look glamorous if it celebrates only the cleverness of the Rasengan. A more convincing reading sees the attack as an action taken in grim necessity, one that should leave Konohamaru with something to think about even if the mission eventually succeeds.

His hesitation afterward matters. The reported scene does not establish that Matsuri is fully defeated; she continues posing a threat despite severe damage. Konohamaru's difficulty following through may look like weak tactical judgment after a carefully planned ambush. But there is also a believable emotional cost to finishing somebody who carries Moegi's face, memories or impulses. The story needs to make the consequences of that cost tangible rather than treat him as foolish for having it. A ninja can know what must be done and still find it difficult to harm a being connected to someone he loves. The chapter's challenge is to respect both parts of that conflict.

Jura's unexpected reaction complicates his theory of emotion

Chapter 19 allowed Jura to speak about love as something the Divine Trees should overcome. In the following installment, contemporary accounts describe him responding emotionally to Matsuri's distress, including a tear. The contrast should not be interpreted too quickly as proof that Jura has become kind or that the enemy is ready to reform. An emotional response does not automatically create empathy, and a creature can suffer while continuing to endanger other lives. The moment is useful because it interrupts his certainty about what emotions are for.

It also changes how readers may understand the connection among the sentient Divine Trees. Matsuri's distress is not entirely private if Jura can perceive or experience something of it. The narrative has not yet supplied a full account of their emotional relationship, and a critic should not fill that gap with invented hive-mind mechanics. What matters is that a supposedly detached observer has been affected by the phenomenon he wanted to analyze. His authority as the group's philosophical voice becomes less secure at the exact moment another member is being hurt through her feelings.

The larger point is that studying a human emotion may require being vulnerable to it. Jura can read about people indefinitely, but witnessing a close connection between pain and affection demands an experience that no amount of abstract reasoning can fully replace. Whether he changes as a result remains uncertain. The chapter's best villain writing lies in making that uncertainty meaningful rather than announcing a moral conversion from one tear.

Ryū's dominance keeps the younger team from winning by numbers alone

The battle with Ryū deteriorates after the loss of Araya's sword. Mitsuki, Sarada, Yodo and Araya can no longer rely on the countermeasure that helped them respond to the Iron Sand. Contemporary descriptions note Ryū immobilizing or manipulating members of the group, with Mitsuki and Araya especially vulnerable to being turned against their companions. The threat works because it is different from a conventional duel. Even when a fighter can summon a powerful technique, somebody else may be used as a shield or made to attack against their will.

Mitsuki's Sage Mode remains significant, and the story does not need to claim his strength has vanished because the enemy gains the upper hand. What the sequence demonstrates is that exceptional speed and chakra are only some of the abilities required for a team to survive. If Ryū controls where people stand and whom they may strike, the strongest individual move can become dangerous. The scene brings attention back to the tactical knowledge the earlier chapters cultivated. Araya's sword had been valuable precisely because it changed the structure of the engagement; losing it gives Ryū the advantage he needs.

The weakness is that the protagonists appear to spend another installment trapped after a strong display of teamwork. A reader may wonder whether the author is repeatedly stripping away their accomplishments simply to create bigger rescues. The danger of that pattern is real. Here, however, the constraint sets the stage for a different character's decision rather than for Boruto to teleport in and solve the problem. That makes the next beat more interesting.

Yodo's offer is heroic, but it should not be romanticized without limits

When Ryū offers the possibility of sparing the fighters in exchange for information about Boruto or Koji, Yodo moves toward sacrificing herself to protect the others. Contemporary analysis describes her proposing to use her abilities to help locate their enemies if her friends can survive. It is a desperate action in a terrible situation. The reader understands why she might consider it: Araya and Mitsuki have been threatened, Sarada has little room to maneuver, and Yodo does not know whether they will live long enough for a rescue.

The offer also exposes the danger of trusting an antagonist's terms. Ryū has already used the group's priorities to remove their best countermeasure, so his willingness to bargain should not be taken as a guarantee. Yodo has agency in making the choice, but it would be simplistic to declare her bravery a complete solution. Her value to the story should extend beyond being the girl willing to sacrifice herself at every crisis. In an ensemble arc, courage needs to coexist with the opportunity to make mistakes, learn, argue and survive for more reasons than somebody else's development.

What makes the moment more interesting is its effect on Sarada. Yodo's insistence that protecting people can involve acknowledging one's own feelings challenges the belief that strong emotion necessarily weakens a shinobi. Sarada has spent part of the mission distracted by an unresolved personal exchange with Sumire. The intervention does not simply cure her insecurity; it gives her another way to interpret that discomfort. A professional can care intensely and still act decisively. The strongest growth comes when the feelings become an acknowledged part of her judgment instead of something she has to suppress before fighting.

Sarada's Mangekyō is a consequential reveal, not a completed rulebook

The climax belongs to Sarada. Contemporary post-release accounts describe her activating her Mangekyō Sharingan and creating a dark, gravitational-looking phenomenon that draws Ryū's Iron Sand toward it. It is a striking reversal after the team has been largely neutralized by that very material. The reveal also pays off a longer narrative commitment: Sarada awakened her Mangekyō earlier while begging Sasuke to protect Boruto, but the time skip has not given her constant, effortless access to a fully explained technique. This encounter provides a new instance of her acting under pressure without making Boruto the immediate rescuer.

The most responsible critical reading stops where the installment stops. Its visual effect resembles a concentrated gravitational pull; the precise rules, costs and limits need further confirmation. The chapter does not establish that Sarada can cancel any ninjutsu anywhere, nor that she can generate the same effect indefinitely without consequence. Those claims would require later evidence. At this point the established dramatic fact is enough: she manifests a power capable of changing the immediate battle conditions, and she does so in defense of companions whose lives depend on her action.

Sarada's development must also be more than a reward for having feelings about Boruto. She cares about him, but she is a ninja who wants to become Hokage, a daughter trying to save Sasuke and a friend whose teammates are being controlled before her eyes. Those motivations should not be flattened into a single romantic explanation. The title Because of Love applies to protective loyalty in several forms, including Yodo's desperate offer and Konohamaru's painful restraint. The strongest interpretation of Sarada's awakening is that she accepts her emotional life without letting it define the entirety of her identity.

Verdict: earned excitement with serious questions left open

Because of Love gives this stretch of the manga a more satisfying center of gravity. Konohamaru acts on the difficult strategy established two chapters earlier, Jura's certainty about emotion cracks, Yodo takes a risk for people she cares about, and Sarada changes the course of the fight when the younger team most needs her. The chapter is exciting not simply because it reveals a new Mangekyō ability, but because it ties that reveal to the consequences of previous teamwork and the refusal to treat affection as a professional defect.

The reservations are just as important. Matsuri survives a devastating attack, which could make Konohamaru's hesitation seem contrived if the story never confronts it; the Sand team spends substantial time incapacitated; and Sarada's power remains uncertain enough that confident claims about its full potential would be misleading. Those weaknesses leave work for subsequent chapters, but they do not erase the value of this turning point.
