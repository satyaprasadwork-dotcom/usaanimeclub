Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 23 Review: The Strong Is Really About Boruto Refusing a Future Written for Him

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 23, The Strong, examined: Jura's advantage, Mitsuki's loyalty, the recovered Thorn Soul Bulbs, Koji's changed prediction and Kawaki's intervention.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Jura wins the immediate contest of strength, but Boruto alters a predicted defeat by persuading Kawaki to change. The most convincing part of the chapter is not the dramatic arrival; it is the evidence that foresight does not cancel free will.

A title about strength arrives when the hero is losing

The Strong sounds like the sort of chapter in which Boruto will finally demonstrate that his three years away have made him untouchable. The actual events undermine that expectation. Contemporary accounts describe Jura repeatedly overwhelming him in close combat after countering the techniques that had worked so well against Code. Boruto can land strikes and continue thinking, but he cannot turn those individual successes into control of the fight. His skill is no longer the chapter's central reassurance. The villain's ability to absorb pressure and return it makes the outcome feel dangerously uncertain.

This is an improvement over battles in which every new encounter exists mainly to showcase a previously unseen trick. An experienced fighter can be extremely capable and still meet somebody who understands the terrain, timing and objectives better. Jura's domination does not mean Boruto has suddenly become weak or that his training was pointless. It means the enemy presents a problem his existing methods do not yet solve. The narrative's best interpretation of strength is therefore not the ability to defeat any opponent on demand, but the capacity to make responsible decisions while facing a loss that seems inevitable.

The distinction is crucial to Kashin Koji's role. An adviser who can see possible futures could make suspense disappear if the protagonist simply performs the safest sequence of actions. Boruto's injuries are a reminder that prediction is not control. He can know a disaster is likely and still struggle to prevent it, particularly when preventing it requires other people to make decisions that belong to them rather than him.

Mitsuki's concern is a choice, not a cured memory

Away from the duel, Konohamaru and Mitsuki try to keep the younger fighters safe and prevent the Thorn Soul Bulbs from being lost. Contemporary coverage notes Mitsuki defeating Claw Grimes in Sage Mode, while Sarada remains unconscious after using Ohirume. His impulse to help Boruto has a particular emotional significance because the world still tells him that the person he once knew as his sun is Kawaki. The change in his behavior since Chapter 7 has not been accompanied by an easy restoration of his prior history. That restraint matters. He does not need a perfect explanation of Omnipotence in order to recognize that somebody is in danger and that he cares what happens to them.

The moment could have offered a better showcase of Mitsuki's fighting abilities, and independent commentary has reasonably questioned whether Sage Mode feels powerful when it is spent dispatching lesser creatures. But that is not the only way a character's worth should be measured. Mitsuki's defining struggle concerns selfhood: can he act according to his own values when the story of his relationships has been manipulated? His willingness to help Boruto without a complete recovery of memory is meaningful precisely because it expresses agency before certainty. A future chapter can strengthen that development by making his choices decisive in battle rather than leaving him mostly adjacent to the protagonist's danger.

The Thorn Soul Bulbs ensure the rescued people still matter

The recovered objects from Ryū and Matsuri are not decorative trophies to be carried away after a battle. One offers a possible route to restore Shinki; the other could release Moegi. Araya's effort to leave with Ryū's Thorn Soul Bulb and Yodo's injured body keeps the Sand Village's human stakes visible even while the main villain dominates the story's attention. The group is trying to preserve the result of a difficult mission rather than simply retreat for its own safety. That makes Boruto's distraction of Jura matter to people who are not in the immediate duel.

The chapter should be read with care where Yodo is concerned. Accounts at this point describe her severe condition, but they do not conclusively establish the eventual outcome. The uncertainty is emotionally appropriate because Araya's responsibility is to seek help, not to narrate a final verdict on the battlefield. A source-backed review should not transform an injured character into a confirmed death based on somebody else's speculation. The same caution applies to the Thorn Soul process. The objects appear capable of restoring victims, but the steps and consequences should remain grounded in what the installment actually establishes.

By keeping those smaller purposes alive, the story shows why a loss against Jura cannot be reduced to a power-scaling embarrassment for Boruto. A ninja can fail to defeat the villain and still succeed in buying time for someone else to recover a loved one. Conversely, a spectacular win that loses the items would leave the mission's central work undone.

Koji sees a different future because somebody else has made a choice

The major development is Kashin Koji encountering a changed possible outcome. Contemporary criticism describes his earlier visions ending with Boruto dead when Jura attacked, making the young ninja's intervention look like a mistake from a purely predictive standpoint. Yet a conversation between Boruto and Kawaki before the current fight altered conditions in a way Koji had not incorporated. Kawaki confronted Amado about his suppressed offensive capabilities, and the resulting change creates a possibility that was absent from the futures Koji previously described.

This is the most persuasive use of Prescience since its introduction. If every later turn of the story is revealed to have been foreseen all along, the device will become an elaborate excuse for the author to withhold information. A prediction that changes because a character makes an unanticipated decision preserves the importance of human agency. Boruto did not merely discover a secret stronger punch; he influenced someone else's choices in a way that could reshape the consequences. Kawaki's willingness to seek an upgrade is hardly a moral transformation, but it alters a practical condition the strategist had assumed would remain fixed.

The scene also makes Koji fallible without making him foolish. A person who can perceive several possible futures does not necessarily know every conversation or every response before it occurs. The world contains independent agents whose behavior changes what is possible. Koji's warning was based on information, and the information has changed. That is the right level of uncertainty to keep his guidance useful without letting him serve as the story's infallible narrator.

Kawaki's arrival is an intervention, not absolution

Kawaki arrives with his offensive restrictions removed, with Delta also present to assist the evacuation effort. Published accounts describe him striking Jura and preventing the immediate killing blow against Boruto, even as the antagonist survives a direct attack that would disable many other enemies. The arrival gives Kawaki a significant purpose in the present conflict. For several installments he had seemed to possess the village's resources and authority without matching the new dangers confronting it. The confrontation finally demonstrates that he can make a tactical difference when his power is no longer limited in the same way.

That does not erase the crimes and choices that put Boruto in exile. Kawaki's desire to protect Naruto remains genuine and frighteningly possessive, and he has shown little willingness to surrender the implanted version of the world that benefits him. A joint fight against Jura is not a reconciliation. It is a recognition that two people who disagree about almost everything can have a reason to prevent an immediate disaster together. The distinction keeps their relationship credible. If the sequel instantly treated the rescue as forgiveness, it would undermine the substantial harm caused by Omnipotence.

Delta's involvement also matters. A battle can be made safer through evacuation and coordination, not just through the presence of another powerful attacker. The story gains value when the supporting cast has practical jobs rather than existing solely to admire a spectacular entrance.

Jura's survival keeps the intervention from becoming a cheap solution

The payoff is deliberately limited. Kawaki's appearance changes the immediate calculation, but Jura is still extraordinarily difficult to injure and can survive conditions that would end a fight with a conventional enemy. The balance of power has shifted just enough to prevent Boruto's expected death, not enough to establish that the heroes have won. That restraint is essential. A story that spends several chapters building Jura as an intelligent existential threat should not erase him because another character has had a laboratory upgrade.

It also makes the following chapter necessary rather than merely optional. Kawaki has opened a small window of time, and Boruto must decide how to use it. He cannot assume that having an ally beside him guarantees a viable attack. The story has finally established that every advantage comes with a different restriction: Sarada's Ohirume exhausts her, Boruto needs focus to teleport, and Kawaki's adjustments create trade-offs within his body. Those limitations create opportunities for strategy in a series that has increasingly seemed capable of outgrowing ordinary tactical conflicts.

Verdict: free will matters more than a dramatic comeback

The Strong is compelling because the protagonist cannot overpower Jura and yet is not entirely helpless. He has acted on a conviction Koji could not reduce to a safe forecast and influenced Kawaki to make a decision that changes the possible outcome. The chapter's title acquires another meaning: strength includes a willingness to accept danger, preserve other people's choices and use an unlikely alliance when the alternative is catastrophe. Jura remains the physically dominant combatant, while Boruto and Kawaki have begun to change the conditions around him.

The weaknesses are visible. Mitsuki's Sage Mode still lacks a decisive demonstration, Kawaki's upgraded entrance could feel convenient if the trade-offs disappear, and the confrontation risks becoming another prolonged display of the antagonist's superiority. But the future-vision reversal supplies meaningful structure behind the spectacle.
