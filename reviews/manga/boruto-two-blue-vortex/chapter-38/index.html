Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 38 Review: Boruto Reaches Sarada, but Kawaki Still Cannot Let Him Live

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 38, Follow Your Heart, reviewed: Kawaki visits the still-sealed Naruto and Hinata, Kobu targets Shikamaru's position, Sarada resists Code and Hidari, Koji warns of Momoshiki, and Boruto finally reaches the hideout.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Follow Your Heart finds a meaningful contrast between Kawaki's one-sided promise to the parents he has imprisoned and Boruto's willingness to risk his own freedom to save Sarada. The final rescue is a tactical success, not the defeat of Code or resolution of the Momoshiki threat.

The title describes a moral risk, not an effortless romantic victory

Follow Your Heart seems at first like a gentle instruction to two friends trying to find one another. Its effect is far more complicated. Boruto is searching for Sarada at the same moment Kashin Koji warns that saving her could contribute to a future in which Momoshiki takes over his body. Kawaki is separately speaking to the parents he has imprisoned, explaining why he still intends to kill anyone he considers an Ōtsutsuki threat. And in the village, Shikamaru is being asked to surrender authority to people whose agenda is increasingly difficult to reconcile with the defense of Konoha. Each character claims to be acting for somebody or something precious, but those commitments lead toward incompatible actions.

The chapter's most interesting point is that following one's heart does not guarantee a morally correct decision. Kawaki is deeply attached to Naruto, yet has turned that attachment into an argument for permanently killing Boruto. Boruto is deeply attached to Sarada, but he may endanger other people and his own autonomy by rushing to save her. Kobu and the officials scrutinizing Shikamaru may invoke responsible government while pursuing a political outcome that weakens the village. The story puts motivations and consequences alongside one another rather than allowing the motive alone to settle the argument.

That complexity is what makes the conclusion compelling. Boruto arrives when Sarada needs him, but the chapter does not close the larger problem. Code has not been fully defeated, Hidari remains unpredictable, and Momoshiki's opportunity has not vanished. The rescue is an achievement with a dangerous future attached, not a final proof that every risky decision was correct.

Kawaki speaks to parents who cannot answer

The chapter opens with Kawaki visiting Naruto and Hinata inside Daikokuten, where they remain unconscious and sealed away. The distinction matters because a glimpse of Naruto in a chapter does not mean he has been released or returned to command the village. Kawaki is able to sit before the two people whose approval means most to him, but they cannot hear him, object or grant forgiveness. The conversation is therefore a confession without the possibility of response. It tells readers more about Kawaki's mental state than about any new agreement within the Uzumaki family.

He apologizes for the duration of their confinement while reaffirming the purpose behind it. Kawaki believes Ōtsutsuki-related beings threaten the peace Naruto wanted to protect and sees eliminating them as necessary. Boruto remains on that list, even though he has repeatedly fought to save the same people. Kawaki insists on protecting Himawari separately from the intended killing of her brother. His loyalty to one member of the family does not cause him to reconsider the harm he is prepared to impose on another. The speech is unsettling because the contradictions are part of his convictions, not a result of momentary confusion.

The scene also makes the consequences of Omnipotence painfully visible. Kawaki occupies a position in the village that should belong to Boruto, yet he has denied himself the possibility of a real family conversation with Naruto and Hinata. Their imprisonment prevents them from challenging his behavior or giving him the acceptance he seems to crave. It is a self-defeating way of expressing love. He preserves their bodies while excluding their judgment from every major decision about their lives.

His promise to protect Himawari is sincere and still insufficient

Kawaki's concern for Himawari is one of his most human motivations. She has begun using Kurama's chakra, has been targeted by Jura and has barely entered the world of dangers surrounding the Divine Trees. His decision to protect her makes emotional sense. The problem is that he believes protection can be separated from the freedom of the person being protected. The same logic that allowed him to seal Naruto and Hinata could make him decide that Himawari's wishes matter less than his view of what is safe.

The chapter does not announce that Kawaki has imprisoned Himawari or will necessarily do so. That remains a possible future fear, not an event established here. What is confirmed is his absolute language about eliminating perceived threats and his reluctance to accept that Boruto's continued existence might also be important to the family. Kawaki's willingness to include himself among the beings whose eventual removal he considers necessary makes the worldview even darker. He appears to imagine peace as something that can arrive only after the people who have caused danger—including himself—are gone.

The strongest interpretation is not that he lacks love. It is that love, fear and the memory of exploitation have become fused with a desire for total control. The story should allow readers to understand those origins while continuing to criticize the decisions they produce.

Kobu's evidence threatens Shikamaru and the system he serves

Back in Konoha, the inquiry into Boruto's escape reaches a more concrete stage. Contemporary accounts describe information recovered from a notebook that Shikamaru used while dealing with the sealing restraints on Boruto. The physical traces can be linked to the code used to release the prisoner, giving Kobu grounds to claim that Shikamaru assisted an accused fugitive. This is an escalation from rumor to evidence that can influence the formal government. The case is dangerous precisely because the factual connection may exist even when the official interpretation of Boruto's identity is false.

Shikamaru cannot solve the problem by announcing the full history of Omnipotence and expecting everyone to accept it. The village's memories have been altered, and his secret reliance on Boruto and Koji would appear suspicious under those memories. The evidence therefore threatens both his political position and the ability to organize resistance against Jura. A leader who possesses the most useful information may lose the authority to act on it because he cannot establish that his information deserves trust. The conflict works as an institutional counterpart to Koji's personal secrecy.

Kobu's motivation is reported as broader than removing one inconvenient Hokage. The argument extends toward reducing or dismantling the traditional power of shinobi villages, using the case against Shikamaru as justification for a political transformation. That is a potentially rich question for a franchise built around military villages. Do the existing institutions serve the public well, or have they preserved dangerous concentrations of authority? The chapter gives the antagonistic officials an agenda, but it should not be treated as proof that ending shinobi institutions is automatically foolish. What makes the present proposal suspect is the apparent willingness to manipulate evidence and install a compliant leader rather than seek an informed public debate.

Konohamaru is a proposed replacement, not an installed Ninth Hokage

The idea of replacing Shikamaru with Konohamaru is alarming because it exploits real relationships and respected history. Konohamaru is connected to the Third Hokage and trained under the Seventh, making him an understandable public candidate. He has also participated in the Sand mission and has a record of personal loyalty to Konoha. Those qualities could make him popular enough for officials to present the succession as an ordinary transition. But the proposal is not the same as a completed appointment. Chapter 38 does not confirm that Konohamaru has taken the office or become the Ninth Hokage.

The more important issue is who would control the replacement once he took the position. If Kobu expects Konohamaru to act as a convenient figurehead while the authorities dismantle village autonomy, the problem is not merely which ninja occupies the chair. It is the relocation of decision-making power away from someone who understands the approaching danger. Konohamaru's own response remains a question for subsequent events, and the chapter should not be made to answer it in advance.

Shikamaru has been carrying a burden that few other officials can comprehend. Replacing him during an existential crisis could make the village vulnerable even if the new leader is honest. At the same time, his secrecy has created a serious accountability problem. A good resolution will have to address both the need for effective command and the danger of asking the public to trust someone whose reasons must remain hidden.

Sarada's confrontation shows why Code's plan was always unstable

At the Divine Trees' hideout, Sarada faces Code and Hidari under conditions none of them controls. Code expects to use her as leverage to force Boruto into a confrontation. Hidari, formed through a process connected to Sasuke, sees her as the person he wishes to consume. Sarada wants to survive, protect her father and exploit the conflict between enemies who cannot fully cooperate. Their goals overlap only by accident. The resulting fight is less a standard two-on-one battle than a series of attempts to redirect the others' attention.

Sarada contributes meaningfully through her physical techniques and tactical choices. Contemporary criticism describes her attacking Code and using Chidori against Hidari while trying not to spend all of her chakra on the taxing Mangekyō ability. That restraint is important after the previous confrontations with Ryū and Mamushi left her exhausted and affected her eyesight. A capable ninja should learn that possessing an extraordinary move is not the same as needing to use it in every exchange. The chapter gives her an opportunity to fight under those limits instead of immediately becoming unconscious after a spectacular attack.

The problem is how much punishment she receives. Code's new appendage-like ability and the geography of the hideout make escape difficult, and the confrontation leaves her badly injured. Readers may reasonably be frustrated by another situation in which Sarada is placed in danger to create urgency for Boruto. That criticism does not erase her effort to maneuver between two enemies. It does make the writer responsible for allowing her choices to retain significance after the rescue arrives.

Hidari's intervention is protective in one sense and possessive in another

One of the chapter's most revealing moments occurs when Code threatens to further mutilate Sarada, including an attack directed at her eyes. Hidari intervenes and stops him. The behavior is striking because Hidari has made Sarada a target himself. Why would a creature that intends to consume someone object to another antagonist harming her? The scene does not establish a simple moral conversion. Hidari may be responding to traces of Sasuke's protective instincts, or he may simply regard Sarada as a target that Code has no right to destroy. The chapter does not conclusively choose between those explanations.

The uncertainty is a strength. Jura's growing affection for Hidari has shown that the Divine Trees can develop attachments with unpredictable consequences. Hidari's reaction could represent a comparable emotional contradiction or a more narrowly possessive impulse. Readers should avoid declaring it proof that Sasuke has regained control from inside him. Sasuke remains trapped, and a single intervention does not restore his identity or make Hidari a trustworthy ally.

For Sarada, the immediate benefit is real. The clash between her attackers creates a window of survival. But a person is not made safe merely because one enemy objects to another enemy's cruelty. Hidari remains dangerous, and the scene's emotional possibilities should not conceal his continued interest in consuming her.

Koji finally stops pretending he can price every outcome

Boruto is struggling to locate the chakra trace Sarada left for him when Koji provides another warning. The strategist now considers the risk of Momoshiki taking control following her rescue especially serious. That danger is linked to the bargain Boruto made earlier, the emotional pressure of the crisis and the unfamiliar future the recent events have created. Momoshiki's internal presence is dramatized through his reaction to the warning, but Chapter 38 does not establish that he has permanently seized Boruto's body. The possibility is urgent, not a completed event.

Koji's response is important because he does not simply forbid Boruto from going. He recognizes that the decision ultimately belongs to the person who must take the risk and encourages him to act according to his own values. After so many scenes in which Koji has treated forecasts as directions, that is a meaningful shift. It does not erase the coercive decisions he made around Inojin and Kobu. It does suggest he has reached the limit of what he can responsibly decide for others.

Boruto's position remains difficult. He wants to save Sarada, who chose to participate in his earlier plan, but his private agreement with Momoshiki means his choices can endanger people beyond the two of them. Courage here is not the absence of uncertainty. It is the willingness to make a decision that has consequences he cannot fully control, while accepting that the burden of those consequences belongs to him.

The arrival is a turning point, not the end of the fight

Boruto finally identifies the connection needed to reach Sarada and teleports into the hideout. He immediately strikes Code, interrupting the assault and delivering the chapter's most satisfying physical response to an enemy whose cruelty has been escalating. The action pays off Sarada's improvised signal in Chapter 37. The earring and the decision to leave a detectable trace mattered because the hero could not simply choose an unknown destination and appear there whenever the story required him.

The scene is gratifying but its scope should remain clear. Chapter 38 does not establish that Code is permanently defeated or that Hidari has been neutralized. It does not show Naruto and Hinata being released, Konohamaru assuming the Hokage office or Momoshiki's threatened takeover reaching a final outcome. The narrative has brought Boruto into the confrontation and changed the immediate balance. The resolution of the larger crisis belongs to subsequent chapters.

The best dramatic consequence is that Boruto and Sarada can now act in the same location, but they must do so after she has been injured and after Koji has identified a new existential risk. A rescue that creates fresh obligations is much more compelling than a rescue that makes every earlier warning meaningless. The final blow against Code should be read as a beginning to the next stage of the fight, not as permission to declare the story resolved.

Verdict: a reunion shadowed by promises that may destroy it

Follow Your Heart succeeds through three contrasting forms of commitment. Kawaki speaks about protecting Naruto and Himawari while refusing to give up a lethal plan against Boruto. Shikamaru protects the village through secret cooperation and faces legitimate questions being weaponized by officials who may want to dismantle the shinobi system. Boruto chooses to save Sarada despite a possible future in which the same decision helps Momoshiki. Each believes that what they care about justifies extraordinary action, but the chapter asks readers to examine what that action does to other people.

The weaknesses are the familiar danger of repeatedly injuring Sarada to motivate her rescue, the crowded movement between political exposition and combat, and the possibility that Koji's warnings will eventually become too flexible to make meaningful predictions. But the chapter's actual progress is clear. Sarada's ingenuity creates a route into the hideout; Boruto arrives because of that choice; and the emotional costs of Kawaki's and Koji's decisions remain unresolved.
