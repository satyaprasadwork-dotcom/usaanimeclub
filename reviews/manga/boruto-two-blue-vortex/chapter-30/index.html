Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 30 Review: Koji's Order Puts Inojin's Conscience Above His Predictions

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 30, All Them Nuisances, analyzed: Team 10's defense against Mamushi, Himawari's Tailed Beast Bomb, Shikadai's shadow cage and Kashin Koji's alarming plan involving Inojin and Kobu.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

The chapter's most frightening moment is not another wave of Mamushi clones. It is Koji telling Inojin to use another person's body in a lethal plan, turning the line between protecting Konoha and sacrificing its people into the real battle.

The arithmetic of Mamushi's bodies creates a civic emergency

All Them Nuisances opens by proving that the previous chapter's victories were not enough. Team 10 has eliminated several Mamushi bodies, but additional copies continue appearing, and the defenders cannot simply assume that a successful strike will end the invasion. The enemy's multiplication means that every exchange must be assessed in terms of what happens after it. A defeated body might not eliminate the original threat, and a single copy moving through a different district could begin another emergency. The premise is stronger than a conventional duel because the number of threats matters as much as the strength of an individual opponent.

This creates a particular challenge for Konoha. Shinobi can face a dangerous opponent on a remote battlefield, but fighting through residential streets puts people and buildings at risk. Mamushi's copies have already shown that they can damage property, whether or not Jura intends the village to remain largely intact. The defenders must account for civilians rather than treating every available technique as safe because it is powerful. That distinction is the foundation of the chapter's most satisfying tactical scene: using the right jutsu in the right place, not merely using the most destructive one.

The continuing multiplication also reinforces the limitations of Koji's earlier briefing. He described the cognitive price of producing more bodies and suggested that certain techniques could control the situation. Yet knowing an opponent's theoretical weakness does not guarantee that a battlefield will behave neatly. A plan must account for confusion, collateral damage, simultaneous targets and the people applying its instructions. Chapter 30's suspense comes from the gap between a useful theory and a crowded village full of independent decisions.

Himawari's power is real; her judgment is still developing

Himawari is eager to protect her teammates and responds to the multiplying enemies by preparing a Tailed Beast Bomb. Contemporary criticism identifies an important problem: an attack with that much destructive force could devastate the area surrounding the Mamushi copies. Her new connection with Kurama grants her extraordinary potential, but it has not automatically supplied the experience needed to use that potential responsibly in a populated city. The chapter therefore gives her another lesson in a form that cannot be solved with a larger chakra reserve.

This is a healthy direction for her character. Chapter 11 established that Himawari could unleash impressive power and still be completely outmatched by Jura's calm control. Since then, the story has shown her trying to understand her abilities with the younger Kurama. Training should involve judgment as much as control of a technique's physical shape. The issue in the present scene is not whether she can produce the attack. It is whether she can predict where its destructive energy will travel and who might be harmed along the way. A young ninja who intends to protect the village can still make an unsafe decision.

Shikadai's response gives the moment its emotional value. He does not simply admire her bravery or assume someone with Kurama's chakra must be right. He recognizes that the attack requires containment and instructs the others accordingly. His willingness to be stern reflects real care for his teammate and the people around them. This makes the series more convincing than an easy celebration of an awakening. Himawari deserves to become a strong fighter, but the path to that goal includes accepting when somebody else's experience should change her plan.

Shikadai and Chocho solve the actual battlefield problem

The tactical solution combines Shikadai's Shadow Cage with Chocho's physical strength. The reported sequence has Shikadai gathering multiple copies inside a shadow-made enclosure and directing Chocho to launch the contained enemies high enough that Himawari can attack without destroying nearby streets. The coordination is specific. Shikadai controls the targets; Chocho moves them; Himawari supplies the finishing power. None of the three actions is an adequate substitute for the others. This is the kind of ensemble combat that made earlier Naruto team formations memorable: a problem is solved by designing a sequence in which each person's specialty changes the options available to everybody else.

Shikadai's altered technique also deserves attention. Contemporary accounts describe a shadow enclosure retaining its form even after the movement takes it away from his immediate contact. That is a meaningful variation on the familiar Nara style because it creates room for more flexible coordination. It should not be extrapolated into the claim that he can maintain unlimited cages anywhere without a cost. The chapter demonstrates one useful application under particular conditions. The important consequence is tactical rather than numerical: his contribution controls where a Tailed Beast Bomb may safely be used.

Chocho's role is equally important. A teammate able to lift and throw the contained enemies solves the spatial problem. It is not enough to have a clever plan if the person assigned to execute it lacks the strength to move the targets. She is not merely providing comic relief or standing nearby to praise someone else's power. Together the three demonstrate that physical force, restraint and precise attack placement can be more effective than any one of those capacities on its own.

The enemy's multiplication makes intelligence work indispensable

Inojin's Mind Transmission Formation continues providing the defenders with information about bodies that can hide chakra signatures. That contribution is not as visually dramatic as Himawari's Tailed Beast Bomb, but the battle would be much harder without it. The team cannot coordinate strikes if it cannot identify where new copies are appearing. Inojin's position links the frontline fighters with the communication network Koji and Shikamaru need to coordinate the entire village. The chapter is therefore dependent on a technique introduced only a few installments earlier, giving the rapid training a concrete payoff.

The story also makes clear that information alone is not victory. Koji can see futures, Inojin can help detect threats and Shikamaru can order teams into position; none of those things guarantees that the people on the ground will have time to react. The expanding number of Mamushi bodies challenges the defenders' capacity to keep up. This is a more substantial use of an information specialist than assigning him a generic ability that makes enemies fall down. His work influences everybody else's chances of being in the right place when another clone appears.

The concern is that Inojin has been pulled into two very different kinds of assignment. The anti-Mamushi sensing network asks him to protect other people through coordination. Koji's next demand will ask him to use his mental techniques in a way that threatens an individual person's life. The chapter's structure deliberately makes those purposes collide.

Kobu is a political obstruction, but obstruction is not a death sentence

Kobu continues investigating the people involved in Boruto's earlier escape, refusing to let the emergency entirely interrupt the inquiry. As an official working with the Fire Country government, he has authority that can complicate Shikamaru's defense plans. Readers understand the frustrating irony. Shikamaru is coordinating against an enemy most of the village cannot handle, while an investigator tries to establish whether he has been collaborating with somebody the public remembers as a traitor. In the story's true history, Shikamaru's conduct may be necessary to save Konoha. In its altered history, the same behavior can look like a serious betrayal.

The chapter invites impatience with Kobu's insistence on the investigation, and that impatience is partly earned. A person who refuses to acknowledge an unfolding crisis because a procedural dispute is more important to his status can make an institution less capable of protecting citizens. Yet an editorial response should resist treating irritation as a sufficient reason to welcome a man's death. Kobu is a nuisance to the protagonists' plan, but he is not therefore a legitimate target for elimination. The distinction becomes crucial when Koji proposes a method for removing him from the political picture.

An intelligent political story would permit Shikamaru to be questioned and still prove competent. It would also allow an investigator to be wrong about the current case without losing the right not to be murdered. The manga is strongest when the crisis exposes the need to examine laws and memories rather than granting an exceptional strategist permission to replace both with personal calculations.

Mind transfer becomes an ethical problem, not merely a clever technique

Contemporary accounts describe Koji directing Inojin to use a remote mind-transfer method through a small toad associated with Shikamaru, taking control of Kobu's body despite the distance from Inojin's location. The technique is a startling development because it stretches familiar Yamanaka methods into a high-risk covert operation. A communication specialist who has been helping detect enemy bodies can now control a person far away. That power could save lives, but it also raises immediate questions about consent, accountability and the dangers of turning somebody into a remotely operated instrument.

The reported plan moves Kobu toward the roof of the Hokage building and creates a confrontation with Mamushi. Koji then orders a lethal outcome, including the possibility of making the controlled official fall or leaving him in the enemy's path. It is important to describe this accurately: the chapter presents the instruction and the desperate position into which Inojin has been placed. It does **not** establish that Inojin has already killed Kobu or completed every part of Koji's proposed plan. That distinction matters when separating the cliffhanger from the events of the following chapter.

More disturbing than the technique is who has been ordered to employ it. Inojin is a teenager recruited because he cares about Himawari and wants to protect Konoha. Now the same motivation is being used to demand an action that might violate the basic duty to preserve life. Koji can argue that allowing Kobu to interfere endangers the world. The existence of a large future threat does not automatically prove that killing this particular person is the only way to respond. The chapter leaves the human decision unresolved, which is precisely why the order is so effective as a cliffhanger.

Sai noticing the deception makes the family cost immediate

Sai's recognition that something is wrong with Kobu creates another layer of tension. He is both a senior ninja experienced in intelligence work and Inojin's father. The apparent manipulation of Kobu's body places those roles in direct conflict. If Sai discovers that his child is carrying out a clandestine operation tied to the Hokage's secret cooperation with Boruto, his response cannot be reduced to a simple choice between family and village. Protecting his son may require challenging the very people commanding the emergency defense. Protecting the village may require understanding why the boy agreed to an ethically dangerous instruction.

That makes Sai a more compelling participant than merely another older character brought back for nostalgia. His experience with an organization that trained children for covert operations gives the situation an especially disturbing resonance. The story does not need to restage every detail of that history to make the parallel meaningful: an authority figure has found a way to turn a young person's loyalty into a willingness to perform a dark assignment. A father who knows the cost of training people to suppress moral judgment has reason to be alarmed.

Inojin's real test is not whether he can complete a powerful jutsu. He has already demonstrated that. It is whether he will remain capable of deciding where obedience must end. The next chapter can either honor his agency or turn him into another person whose feelings exist only to justify the plans of a more informed adult.

Verdict: the most dangerous nuisance is unchecked certainty

All Them Nuisances has two very different kinds of action that ultimately ask the same question: how should people use power when others may pay for the consequences? Himawari has the power to destroy several Mamushi copies, but her teammates must help her use it without harming civilians. Shikadai, Chocho and Inojin make a persuasive team because each recognizes a different constraint. Koji, meanwhile, possesses overwhelming strategic information and uses it to justify an instruction that threatens the life of a political opponent. The contrast is sharp. The younger fighters cooperate to reduce the cost of victory; the clairvoyant strategist appears prepared to transfer the cost onto somebody else.

The chapter's weakness is that the multiplication threat remains repetitive, while Kobu's antagonism is written so insistently that some readers may be encouraged to dismiss his humanity before Koji's plan is revealed. But the cliffhanger works when read as a test of the protagonist's moral world rather than an invitation to applaud a villainous order. It leaves Inojin with a choice more consequential than learning another technique. It does not claim to have read every restricted panel firsthand, invent an outcome for the cliffhanger or assign a fabricated score.
