Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 21 Critical Response: Sarada's Ohirume Wins the Battle, but the Cost Is Not Hers Alone

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 21, Mangekyō Sharingan, examined: Sarada's Ohirume, Sasuke's trust, Ryū's defeat, Yodo's condition and Boruto's decision to defy Kashin Koji.

Source-based commentary by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Sarada's decisive action finally gives her a victory she has earned, but the chapter refuses an entirely comfortable celebration: Yodo's condition and Koji's possible-future calculations ask who pays for a useful awakening.

Sarada's most important breakthrough precedes the fight

Chapter 20 stopped at the moment Sarada began using an unfamiliar power. Chapter 21 finally treats the ability as something more than a tease. Contemporary accounts describe a sequence of memories involving Boruto, her father and her ambition to become Hokage. They explain why a person who wishes to lead the village might struggle to admit that personal attachment matters to her. Sarada has been trying to appear composed and independent while living through a collective lie about someone she knows is innocent. Her emotions are neither a shameful distraction nor a guarantee of good judgment. They are evidence of relationships she refuses to discard simply because the majority believes a different account.

This distinction matters more than which eye technique she acquires. A Hokage needs political and moral judgment rather than just the strongest jutsu in a crowded battlefield. Sarada's loyalty to Boruto was already a significant decision after Omnipotence. Her problem now is not whether she can confess a crush but whether she can accept that protecting other people is compatible with the responsibility she wants to carry. The chapter becomes less interesting whenever that conflict is reduced to romance alone. She is also the daughter of Sasuke, a teammate watching people suffer and a prospective leader whose village may be defending a false history. All of those facts should inform her growth.

Sasuke's trust supplies a reason that cannot be reduced to bloodline talent

The flashback to Sasuke is an important reminder of why Boruto escaped Konoha in the first place. Accounts of the chapter describe him noticing Sarada's Mangekyō and trusting her urgency despite the contradiction between her warning and his altered memories. He did not regain a complete, reliable history before making his choice. He was willing to treat his daughter's judgment as evidence that his own understanding might be compromised. That is a strong continuation of Sasuke's characterization: a man once prepared to sever relationships now risks everything because he believes somebody close to him.

The scene also offers a convenient explanation for Sarada's sudden familiarity with her ability. According to the reported discussion, Sasuke expected her to understand something of its operation instinctively. This keeps the manga from devoting another installment to a training sequence, but it presents a problem. A powerful technique is more compelling when its user discovers boundaries through action and errors. An inherited aptitude need not erase the need for experience. Sarada can know how to begin using an ability without understanding its long-term physical price, limitations or every possible application. The chapter would be stronger still if subsequent scenes made that difference visible.

Ohirume gives Sarada a distinct tactical identity

The reported battle identifies Sarada's ability as Ohirume. Rather than reproducing Sasuke's Chidori at a larger scale, her Mangekyō generates dark gravitational spheres that alter how matter and attacks move through the battlefield. Ryū's Iron Sand, previously a method of trapping the entire team, becomes something Sarada can pull away, redirect or use to restrict the Divine Tree's options. Contemporary critical responses describe several spheres and the ability to choose what is affected. The successful attack ends Ryū's immediate threat, making the recovery of Shinki possible. This is an ensemble payoff rather than an arbitrary fight: the Sand team's earlier attempt failed, and Sarada changes the conditions under which that conflict can be fought.

Her ability is visually and narratively distinct, but the chapter should not be stretched into a universal power-ranking claim. Showing that she can influence Ryū's Iron Sand does not prove she can ignore all defenses, eliminate every form of ninjutsu or maintain those gravitational fields without limit. Accounts of the encounter describe intense exhaustion after the attack, followed by unconsciousness. That cost matters enormously. A technique powerful enough to win one battle needs circumstances under which it cannot simply solve every later battle. Sarada has earned the right to be formidable without becoming an effortless replacement for every other member of Team Seven.

Yodo's condition makes a victory uneasy

The other young ninja do not emerge from the confrontation untouched. Yodo has suffered serious injuries, and the immediate accounts do not provide reassuring certainty that she will recover. Her predicament deserves more than a sentence written solely to increase the stakes for Sarada. Yodo joined the mission because Shinki was in danger, and her partnership with Araya and the Sand team gave her a reason to fight independent of anybody from Konoha. When a supporting character suffers so that a more prominent character can reach an emotional breakthrough, a manga risks treating a person as an expendable prompt for somebody else's development.

The more troubling issue is Kashin Koji's position. Published criticism discusses a possible future in which Sarada's awakening was strategically valuable even though an individual might be lost. A strategist who can view multiple futures has reasons to think about consequences over a long horizon. That does not grant him uncontested moral authority over who should be sacrificed. The person suffering may never have consented to take such a role in his calculations. Chapter 21 is strongest when it lets Boruto resist the idea that saving a great many people makes every smaller loss easy to accept. The outcome might be necessary, but necessity is not the same as permission to stop caring.

Boruto's choice is the first serious test of Koji's guidance

Boruto learns that Konohamaru's situation remains dangerous. Koji warns that intervening will draw Jura toward him and jeopardize a route that the strategist considers preferable. Boruto nevertheless moves to save his former teacher and strikes the already wounded Matsuri before the creature can finish him. The action is not framed as a painless triumph. He has been told that a powerful enemy may follow, and earlier chapters have established that he cannot simply solve every problem through teleportation. The choice is morally intelligible precisely because it involves risk to himself and potentially to the larger mission.

This marks a necessary distinction between foresight and command. Koji can describe situations Boruto may otherwise fail to anticipate; he cannot replace Boruto's responsibilities with a mathematical instruction. The protagonist could have obeyed the apparently safer plan and preserved his strength. Instead, he behaves like someone who regards people as ends rather than variables in a prediction. That choice may prove costly. It is valuable that the manga does not immediately reward him with an effortless win. The tension comes from asking whether a compassionate decision remains defensible when it causes a terrible strategic complication.

Verdict: an earned victory and an unpaid moral debt

Mangekyō Sharingan offers Sarada the chapter she has needed since the time skip. Ohirume alters the battle through her own decisions, while the Sasuke flashback connects that strength to an earlier act of trust. She defeats a threat that genuinely endangered her companions, and the action provides a potential route to save Shinki. It is a significant improvement over scenes that left her waiting for Boruto to intervene. At the same time, the technique's exhaustion creates a necessary limitation and a reason for continued growth.

The chapter is less persuasive when it relies on a sudden explanation of intuitive Mangekyō mastery or seems willing to treat Yodo's possible death as an acceptable cost of Sarada's breakthrough. Boruto's refusal to abandon Konohamaru supplies a compelling disagreement with Koji's calculations, but his arrival also threatens to shift attention away from Sarada too quickly. These tensions do not cancel the result; they make it more consequential. This is an original, source-based critical assessment supported by VIZ, Shueisha and contemporary reviews, not a fabricated firsthand appraisal of every restricted manga panel or an invented rating.
