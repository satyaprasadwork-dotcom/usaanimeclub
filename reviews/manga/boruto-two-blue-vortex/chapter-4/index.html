Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 4 Review: New Divine Trees Make Code Look Small, and Sasuke's Fate Hurts More

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 4, The Awakening, examined: Flying Thunder God, the sentient Divine Trees, Kashin Koji, Sasuke's condition and the dangers of Code's lost control.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

The Awakening succeeds by changing the enemy's nature rather than merely adding strength. The sentient trees carry stolen human connections into the threat, and Sasuke's condition gives the revelation an immediate emotional cost.

Flying Thunder God is impressive for the right reason

Boruto's pursuit of Code pays off with a technique instantly recognizable to longtime Naruto readers: Flying Thunder God, associated with his grandfather Minato. Contemporary reporting describes Boruto using the toad-assisted lead to reach the dimension containing Code's Ten-Tails. It would be easy to treat that as a legacy applause line, the kind of inherited signature move included because fans remember the original series. The more interesting detail is Boruto's admitted imperfection. He can use an extraordinary space-time technique without having mastered it to Minato's level, which preserves a sense that a famed predecessor's achievement was not trivial.

The ability also changes the story's geography. Claw Marks and strange dimensions have previously offered the villains an advantage because a fight could be conducted somewhere ordinary shinobi could not follow. Flying Thunder God begins to close that distance. Boruto can pursue a larger plan instead of waiting for enemies to appear. That is a meaningful change in agency, even before the strength of the technique is considered. The scene provides the launch point for an unpleasant irony: reaching the hidden place is not the same as reaching the threat in time. A powerful arrival can make a failure feel worse, because escape and ignorance were no longer sufficient explanations.

The Ten-Tails is no longer just a weapon

At Code's hideout, Boruto discovers that the Ten-Tails has changed. The official VIZ description of Volume 2 identifies the new entities as Divine Tree beings possessing their own consciousness. Contemporary Chapter 4 coverage describes several figures emerging with human-like characteristics and dangerous abilities. This is a much more consequential development than a numerical increase in enemy strength. Code had treated Claw Grimes as instruments he could direct. Now the thing that should have obeyed him is capable of independent interest and judgment, and those interests need not include loyalty to its maker.

The most unsettling feature is curiosity. A creature whose only purpose is to consume can be handled as an obstacle; a creature that wants to learn about itself can choose when and how to act. The trees retain a destructive instinct, but consciousness gives that instinct a personality. It becomes possible for them to disagree, observe, and prioritize targets for reasons not reducible to the villain's original plan. That prospect immediately makes Code less interesting as the main threat. His recklessness has produced something he cannot understand. Rather than elevating him into the final boss, the chapter makes him the first person dramatically outgrown by his own weapon.

Sasuke's absence is finally given a disturbing shape

Chapter 4's Sasuke-shaped enemy is a shock, but the deeper injury lies in the explanation. Contemporary criticism records the revelation that Sasuke himself is trapped within a tree. The new sentient being resembles him because of the connection to a victim. The chapter does not turn that discovery into a complete account of when Sasuke was caught or how to release him. Instead it lets the revelation reframe Boruto's preceding three years: the sword, the discipline and the solitude are no longer simply evidence of off-screen training. They are traces of a relationship interrupted by disaster.

That approach is emotionally powerful and also risky. Sasuke is a beloved character, and using his likeness as a monstrous reveal can become cheap shock if the story never explores what the loss means for Sarada, Sakura and Boruto. Here, the implication is serious enough to warrant attention. The person Sarada persuaded to protect her friend is himself helpless, while a hostile copy appears able to use familiar techniques. The narrative has converted mentorship into a threat of distorted inheritance: Boruto carries Sasuke's lessons, yet his enemy may carry a grotesque imitation of his abilities. Those two forms of legacy are now set against each other.

Kawaki and Sarada are still fighting over the wrong category

Back in Konoha, reports of the chapter describe a clash between Sarada and Kawaki over Boruto and the danger posed by Ōtsutsuki. Sarada points toward a contradiction: Kawaki treats Boruto's Ōtsutsuki nature as a reason he must die while that description also applies to Kawaki himself. Kawaki's stance implies a willingness to place his own survival under the same harsh rule eventually, but a stated principle is not a tested decision. It is precisely the kind of absolute moral formula that works comfortably until somebody asks who must enforce it and on whom.

The conversation fits the awakening of the Divine Trees more closely than it first appears. The village has reduced Boruto to a category, traitor and dangerous Ōtsutsuki, while Code reduced the Ten-Tails to a category, controllable monster. Both assumptions prevent characters from seeing what is actually in front of them. Sarada insists on judging Boruto through conduct and memory she trusts; Code refuses to imagine that his creations can develop interests of their own. The parallels do not make the cases morally identical, but they give the chapter a useful theme: dangerous decisions begin when labels replace attention.

A dramatic turn that also exposes the pacing problem

Reports of Boruto's meeting with the new enemies describe how quickly he is overwhelmed and forced to withdraw, with Kashin Koji revealed as an ally who can call him out for taking risks. This restores a necessary uncertainty after Uzuhiko. Boruto's newfound capability has not made him the strongest actor in every room. The four sentient trees create a threat that neither his speed nor Code's surviving machinery seems ready to handle. There is real narrative relief in seeing the protagonist encounter something outside his preparation, because a compelling sequel needs more than a succession of demonstrations that its hero trained effectively.

Nevertheless, the introduction is crowded. Several startling designs, an unfamiliar rule set, a Sasuke revelation, a new alliance and continuing village politics all compete for attention in one monthly installment. The chapter favors momentum over emotional processing. Its best scenes open questions, not settle them, and that is both its appeal and its limitation. Viewed through publisher summaries and contemporary criticism rather than a claimed direct reading, The Awakening stands as the point where Two Blue Vortex moves beyond revenge between two boys. The challenge for the following chapter is to give these new entities motivations and consequences rather than treating them as impressive silhouettes.
