Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 17 Review: The Cruelty of Shikamaru's Plan Makes the Divine Trees More Than Targets

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 17, Wild Beast Cubs, examined: Shikamaru's order to deceive Matsuri, the Ryū separation plan, Jura's peaceful bookstore visit, Sarada's distractions and Kawaki's demand for an upgrade.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

The strongest tension is ethical rather than physical: Konoha plans to manipulate beings whose emotions are new, unstable and dangerous. The suspense comes from whether a clever plan that uses affection as bait can survive contact with people who refuse to behave predictably.

The title makes an ethical claim the characters cannot escape

Wild Beast Cubs is a strange way to describe beings that could destroy trained shinobi with almost no effort. That mismatch is the point. As contemporaneous critical accounts describe, Shikamaru characterizes the sentient Divine Trees as dangerous young animals: extremely powerful, curious and inexperienced with the human emotions beginning to appear inside them. They were not raised among the village's social obligations and do not share a human adult's understanding of harm. Their vulnerability to manipulation is therefore part of the tactical problem. But the label also risks giving the heroes permission to treat living minds as objects for disposal.

Shikamaru does not propose negotiating endlessly until the Sand Village is destroyed. The plan is to divide Matsuri and Ryū, obtain their Thorn Soul Bulbs and restore Moegi and Shinki. It has a human objective worth defending. The harsh part is the proposed method: exploit Matsuri's unexplained attraction to Konohamaru, gain her trust and kill her while she is vulnerable. The dilemma becomes sharper precisely because she is not harmless. Her ability to kill does not automatically settle whether every form of betrayal is justified, especially if the beings may be developing a kind of personhood. Chapter 17 earns its dramatic weight by leaving both sides of that problem visible.

The story's most uncomfortable possibility is that the plan saves two innocent people by encouraging a professional ninja to perform a calculated emotional betrayal. Naruto's legacy has often depended on recognizing personhood in enemies who were treated as monsters. Shikamaru is not Naruto, and the world facing him may not permit the same options. He should be allowed to make a hard choice without being simplified into either a coward or a visionary. The test is whether he understands what his own order means. His willingness to acknowledge its cruelty makes the operation more credible than a scene in which everybody celebrates manipulating a naive creature.

Konohamaru's relationship to Moegi is the center of the trap

Konohamaru approaches the Divine Trees with Sarada and Mitsuki under the pretense of pursuing the fugitive Boruto. The deception has practical value: Ryū and Matsuri have reasons to distrust the village, and the Shinju do not yet understand every social cue. When Matsuri meets Konohamaru, her reaction changes. Reports describe visible confusion and an unexpectedly affectionate response linked to the human Moegi, whose chakra helped produce her. Ryū steps in to handle the conversation as Matsuri struggles with emotions she cannot name. The team's plan depends on treating those feelings as an exploitable opening.

Konohamaru's position is substantially harder than it might look from the outside. Moegi is a friend and former teammate. Matsuri shares a link to her and exhibits aspects of her attachment without simply being Moegi. If he responds warmly, it could further confuse the creature while buying time to rescue the original person. If he recoils, he could trigger an attack that destroys both the mission and the chance of recovering Moegi. This is a more credible test of his maturity than another opportunity to overpower a stranger. His choices carry both professional and personal costs.

The weakness is the amount of characterization left to implication. Readers need enough understanding of Konohamaru's own feelings to feel the depth of his conflict. A plot can tell us that Matsuri is drawn to him, but that is not a substitute for sustained scenes showing why Moegi matters to him independently of the crisis. Chapter 17 provides the framework. It does not yet deliver the full emotional account. The potential is real, but the writer must make the people involved more than switches that activate a tactical plan.

Sarada, Mitsuki, Yodo and Araya make a necessary ensemble

The smaller team escorts Ryū toward the Sand Village fighters while Konohamaru occupies Matsuri. The goal is to create separate conflicts rather than accept an unwinnable simultaneous battle against two enemies. Sarada and Mitsuki are capable ninja, but this operation is the first meaningful opportunity to watch them cooperate with Yodo and Araya under the stakes created by Shinki's imprisonment. The Sand characters bring knowledge of their lost teammate; the Leaf characters bring different techniques and the consequences of their own history. That creates the possibility of genuine teamwork instead of a procession of unrelated rescues.

Yodo and Araya also give the emergency a local perspective. Shinki's loss is not only a problem for international relations or Gaara's leadership. It is the disappearance of somebody who trained, argued and took risks alongside them. Their urgency can produce impatience, especially when strangers arrive with a plan that asks them to manipulate an enemy instead of fighting directly. That difference in priorities makes the operation more believable. The best version of this arc will let the Sand team contribute knowledge that the Leaf team could not possess, rather than allowing the visitors to dominate every decision.

Sarada's attention is divided after the conversation with Sumire. That detail can work as emotional continuity: an unresolved disagreement should not disappear because the plot has moved to a new country. It also poses a writing risk. If her distraction exists chiefly so that the male characters can rescue her later, the story will have weakened one of the very people whose independence it has spent years establishing. The tactical and emotional strands must reinforce each other. A character can care deeply about a friend while still performing her work competently, and her mistakes should arise from recognizable choices rather than a need to manufacture suspense.

Boruto's absence makes the outcome less certain

According to contemporary accounts, Koji's preferred scenario depends on Konohamaru and the younger fighters handling the mission without Boruto's immediate intervention. If Boruto appears, Jura may respond, and the possible future deteriorates. That warning is useful because it keeps the protagonist's enormous gains from the time skip from solving every new problem. It also allows Sarada, Mitsuki and the Sand team to show what they can do under conditions that make victory unlikely. The chapter asks readers to care about the teamwork rather than merely wait for Boruto's next entrance.

But Koji's forecast has an ethical blind spot. A strategy that increases the likelihood of the desired outcome is not the same as a guarantee that everybody involved survives. Even if the team saves Shinki and Moegi, what losses might make that result possible? The existence of multiple futures cannot absolve the strategist of the duty to acknowledge human costs. Boruto's question about those costs is more interesting than the familiar frustration of being told to stay away from a fight. He has been forced to trust somebody who can provide probabilities without allowing the participants to know what they are risking.

Jura in a bookstore is the chapter's most disturbing kind of normal

The appearance of Jura in a Konoha bookstore should be a comic interlude, and it is funny in a grim way. An entity capable of terrifying the village behaves as an ordinary customer, while the authorities understand that provoking him could bring disaster. Shikamaru's instruction to leave him alone is a decision grounded in survival. The scene does not mean Jura has become harmless or that buying books counts as moral growth. It makes him more alarming because he is willing to learn about people without accepting the ethical restraints that give human social life its meaning.

Reading is an appropriate pastime for a villain defined partly by curiosity. Jura can absorb information, appreciate a bookstore and still regard a person as something to consume. That contrast distinguishes intelligence from empathy. It also places Konoha in a new predicament: the village cannot reliably distinguish a peaceful visit from the opening of a catastrophe. Ordinary policing becomes inadequate when the presence of one customer requires the leadership to consider the future of everyone on the street.

Verdict: the hardest part of the plan is living with it

Wild Beast Cubs is a deliberate setup chapter, but its questions are unusually sharp. Shikamaru gives Konohamaru a mission built on betrayal; Matsuri begins experiencing emotions she cannot explain; Ryū accepts a false premise for reasons the heroes do not fully understand; and four younger ninja position themselves for a fight that cannot be won through reckless strength alone. The chapter's best accomplishment is making the coming battle a dispute about deception, vulnerability and the use of privileged knowledge. Even a successful ambush might leave its participants with uncomfortable memories.

The main weakness is that the operation needs later chapters to deliver much of its payoff. Sarada's emotional distraction may feel overly convenient, Konohamaru's connection to Moegi remains underexplored, and Koji's favorable future can make decisions seem predetermined. Nevertheless, the narrative now possesses something more compelling than another generic encounter. The heroes may have to save people by betraying an enemy who is developing personlike feelings.
