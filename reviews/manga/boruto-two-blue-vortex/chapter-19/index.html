Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 19 Review: Konohamaru's Worst Weapon Is Someone Else's Affection

Boruto: Two Blue Vortex Chapter 19, Konohamaru-chan, critically examined: the history of Araya's blade, the battle with Ryū, Matsuri's anger, Jura's argument against love and the Sand team's dangerous loss of its countermeasure.

Source-based review by USA Anime Club Editorial · Research checked November 15, 2026 · Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Spoiler notice: this article discusses released installment events described by the sources.

THE SOURCED TAKEAWAY

Chapter 19 is effective when it gives teamwork a real tactical history and asks whether exploiting affection can ever remain a clean strategy. The stakes rise without relying on a surprise power-up, although Konohamaru's apparent passivity strains credibility.

The best part of the Iron Sand fight happened before it began

Konohamaru-chan opens with a flashback about an object that mattered at the end of the preceding chapter: Araya's sword. Post-release coverage describes Shinki helping Araya develop a weapon capable of interacting with Iron Sand, using his own chakra to make the technique viable. The collaboration was part of their effort to become more effective defenders of Gaara. This is excellent groundwork because it gives the weapon significance beyond a convenient counter created just in time for Ryū's debut. It originated in a relationship between people who trusted one another before one of them became trapped by a Divine Tree.

That history also changes how the audience should interpret Araya's present predicament. He is using knowledge and tools developed with Shinki against an entity created from Shinki's chakra. The fight is not simply a bizarre mirror match. It tests whether the experience of knowing the original person can provide a meaningful advantage over a creature that inherits some of that person's abilities. The answer is provisional, but it is encouraging. A sword built through cooperation can interrupt an enemy who uses borrowed powers for domination. The same shared history that makes the creature emotionally frightening is also the source of the heroes' resistance.

The flashback gives Shinki character even while he is absent. His dedication to protecting Gaara is familiar, but his willingness to assist Araya with a new technique suggests he values collective strength rather than assuming he must shoulder the defense of the Sand alone. Araya, meanwhile, emerges as more than a quiet figure standing next to Yodo. The sequel needs exactly this sort of continuity for its supporting cast. A character becomes worth following when their prior choices affect what they can do now. The exposition is comparatively economical because the blade has already been introduced; the flashback reveals the relationship behind it instead of announcing another arbitrary power.

Teamwork turns a terrifying advantage into a solvable problem

In the present confrontation, Araya's blade helps the Leaf and Sand fighters manipulate or disrupt Ryū's Iron Sand. Mitsuki attacks in Sage Mode, Sarada looks for openings, and Yodo uses her heightened sensory abilities to track movements that would otherwise remain hidden. Contemporary reviews describe the group briefly gaining ground by combining these roles. This is more convincing than making one member of the team suddenly equal the Divine Tree in raw output. Each person offers information or a counter the others lack. The enemy remains dangerous, but the heroes can influence the terms of the engagement.

That arrangement directly rewards the political decision to send an international team. Konohamaru may have been chosen because of Matsuri's attachment, yet Araya and Yodo are not interchangeable local guides. Their experience with Shinki shapes the fight against Ryū. Sarada and Mitsuki contribute their own training and adapt to a style of combat different from the threats facing Konoha. If the scene were resolved by Boruto teleporting in to deliver the final attack, the preceding chapters' efforts to establish this group would feel redundant. Allowing them to work through a complicated enemy is an important creative choice.

The success is limited, which is necessary. Ryū does not respond to the counter by standing still and permitting defeat. He has his own way of changing the battlefield. The ability to transform into Iron Sand and reposition through Claw Marks means the heroes must account for multiple kinds of movement and surprise. A scene with a functioning countermeasure becomes suspenseful when the enemy begins trying to remove it, not when the writer invents a larger number for everybody's chakra. This is where a tactical manga can be more compelling than an endless ladder of transformations.

Ryū's use of the Claw Grimes is cleverer than it looks

The turning point comes when Ryū releases Claw Grimes to overwhelm and distract the shinobi. An attack on Yodo draws Araya into a protective response, and the enemy uses the confusion to send away the sword that has been undermining his Iron Sand. Contemporary accounts describe a Claw Grime taking the weapon as the opponent removes both from immediate reach. This is an effective reversal because it arises from the specific plan the heroes have developed. Ryū identifies which tool is causing trouble and targets it rather than simply trying to overpower everyone simultaneously.

The action reveals another unsettling feature of the sentient Divine Trees. They are new minds, but they can study their enemies' dependencies quickly. Shikamaru was right that their emotional development might make them vulnerable, but assuming that inexperience extends to all tactical situations would be a dangerous mistake. Ryū can exploit concern for a teammate without fully sharing that concern. He may not have lived among humans for years, but he has enough intelligence to recognize that the people fighting him will act to protect one another.

Yodo's vulnerability is also a reminder that the mission has human stakes. Her sensory skills are not a magical barrier against injury. Araya's decision to shield her is consistent with the personal history the flashback has just supplied. The story benefits from allowing that care to produce a mistake. In a fight, affection can make people predictable, and predictable actions are exploitable. That is not an argument that friendship is a weakness worth abandoning. It is an argument that bonds carry risks alongside their immense tactical value. The enemy's victory consists in using an otherwise admirable reaction to remove the team's best answer.

Konohamaru faces the emotional history he wanted to keep private

The other confrontation grows increasingly unstable as Matsuri reacts to Konohamaru. She calls him by the familiar nickname associated with Moegi, and his objection causes the encounter to change. Published readings describe Matsuri interpreting the rejection through jealousy, jealousy she may barely understand. She has inherited a connection to someone from Konohamaru's childhood without inheriting that person's entire life. Her attraction makes her vulnerable to manipulation, but her power turns that vulnerability into a danger for anyone close to her.

Konohamaru's discomfort is understandable. Allowing a being formed from Moegi's chakra to use a private mode of address can feel like permitting a stranger to impersonate someone he desperately wants to save. At the same time, treating Matsuri only as an impostor ignores the possibility that her feelings are real experiences for the being who has them. She is not Moegi; that does not automatically mean she feels nothing. The ethical tension begun in Chapter 17 deepens because the deception now operates through a bond the hero is struggling to manage himself.

The problem is his apparent lack of an effective defensive response as Matsuri's anger becomes physical. A seasoned ninja facing an unpredictable enemy ought to retain at least one contingency, especially after being specifically briefed about her attraction. The narrative might intend to show him hesitating because she resembles Moegi. That emotional paralysis has dramatic potential, but readers deserve a clearer sense of what he is risking or calculating. Otherwise a legitimate inner conflict begins to look like a character standing motionless because the plot needs him captured.

Jura's theory of love is the chapter's coldest idea

Jura's conversation with the other Divine Trees offers a philosophical counterpart to the mission. As contemporary criticism notes, he interprets human love as a force capable of overriding rational decisions. His ambition is to understand the feeling and perhaps overcome it rather than be weakened by it. This is not necessarily an insightful diagnosis just because it comes from an intelligent creature. A person can make a harmful decision out of love, but people also form communities and protect strangers through care that exceeds narrow self-interest. Jura's theory explains some behavior while failing to account for why a bond is worth sustaining.

The contrast with Araya is productive. Araya's concern for Yodo permits Ryū to exploit an opening, but the same network of relationships is why the team possesses a sword built through Shinki's cooperation. Love may create vulnerability, yet it has also produced the heroes' only credible means of opposing the enemy. Treating the first fact as proof that love must be discarded ignores the second. The narrative's strength lies in making Jura's argument sound plausible for a moment and then letting the larger story challenge its completeness.

His theory also reflects the Divine Trees' own confusion. Matsuri cannot easily separate wanting closeness from wanting to consume Konohamaru. Ryū can copy human tactics while lacking a moral commitment to the humans affected. Jura can read books and reason about social bonds but remains indifferent to the violence required by his plans. Their intelligence is developing faster than their responsibility. The story need not decide instantly whether they can become ethical beings. It should resist confusing intellectual sophistication with wisdom.

Verdict: an effective tactical defeat with one frustrating hesitation

Konohamaru-chan rewards readers for paying attention to supporting relationships. Araya's blade matters because of a previous collaboration with Shinki; the Sand and Leaf fighters become stronger through combined specialties; Ryū responds by targeting the countermeasure instead of waiting to lose. The scene gives the enemy a believable tactical success and makes the group vulnerable without suddenly discarding their training. Meanwhile, Matsuri's use of Moegi's intimate nickname turns an infiltration plan into a painfully personal encounter.

The weaknesses are significant enough to note. Konohamaru's stillness is hard to reconcile with his experience unless the manga eventually gives his hesitation more psychological clarity. Yodo risks being used as a danger signal instead of receiving sustained independent development. And Jura's theorizing can feel distant from the immediate crisis even when its themes are relevant. Those concerns do not cancel the installment's strongest achievement: the villains win ground by understanding how the heroes care for one another.
