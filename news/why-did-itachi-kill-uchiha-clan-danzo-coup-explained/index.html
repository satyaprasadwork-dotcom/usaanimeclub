Why Did Itachi Kill the Uchiha Clan? Danzō, the Coup and the Full Truth

Why did Itachi kill the Uchiha clan and spare Sasuke? Understand the planned coup, Danzō's ultimatum, Konoha's responsibility and the tragic aftermath.

桜
USA Anime Club Editorial

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

QUICK ANSWER

Itachi killed most of the Uchiha clan while trapped between a planned Uchiha coup, the Hidden Leaf's leadership and Danzō's coercive plan to prevent rebellion. He spared Sasuke and accepted the role of a hated criminal. The later revelation explains his motives; it does not erase the victims, absolve the village or mean there was no other morally imaginable choice.

WHAT TO CHECK NEXT

Who planned the coup, who imposed the ultimatum, and who committed the killings?

Separate the Uchiha political crisis, Danzō's coercion, Itachi's decision and Sasuke's consequences. Understanding a motive is not an endorsement of the act.

Before the explanation: a spoiler boundary worth respecting

The answer involves one of Naruto's defining reversals. Early in the series, Sasuke tells a story about a brilliant older brother who annihilated their family and left him alive. Viewers have every reason to accept the apparent outline. Later material reveals political surveillance, a threatened coup and a village leadership that treats one young shinobi as expendable. If you have not reached Sasuke's confrontation with Itachi and its aftermath, the rest of this article will disclose that turn directly.

The simplest accurate answer is that Itachi carried out the Uchiha massacre to prevent what he believed would be a disastrous rebellion, under pressure from Danzō and the village's security apparatus, while arranging to spare his little brother. But a simple sentence can quietly make a terrible event sound inevitable. The real value of examining this story is separating what happened, what Itachi believed he was preventing, and who benefited from keeping Sasuke in the dark.

The problem began before Itachi was given a mission

The Uchiha were not an isolated family who suddenly decided to attack their neighbors. Tensions between their clan and Konoha had accumulated, fed by suspicion and unequal political treatment. Their grievance matters because it explains why a faction began considering a coup, even though planning an armed seizure of power carried obvious dangers for everyone in the village. The official Naruto anniversary retrospective explicitly discusses the discrimination and political history behind the Uchiha decision. A reading that skips this background reduces a conflict between institutions to one family's supposed disloyalty.

Itachi occupied the worst possible position within this situation. His father Fugaku was central to the clan's leadership, while the village recruited Itachi's talents into its security structure. From inside the family he was expected to supply information about the Leaf; from inside the Leaf's institutions he was expected to report on the Uchiha. It is tempting to call him a double agent and imagine glamour or freedom. What he had was competing authority, almost no safe room to dissent, and a younger brother whose life could become leverage.

What exactly did Danzō do?

Danzō represented the harshest answer to the prospect of rebellion: eliminate the perceived threat before it could become open fighting. In the Itachi Shinden material, the approach hardens into a terrible bargain involving Sasuke's survival. This is why the familiar shorthand, 'the Hokage ordered Itachi to kill everyone,' is not precise enough. Hiruzen's efforts toward negotiation, Danzō's unilateral and coercive methods, and other leaders' participation in an abusive security structure should not be flattened into one person speaking for every official at every moment.

The Naruto official Shisui retrospective adds an essential earlier step: Danzō stole Shisui's right eye, disrupting a possible intervention through Kotoamatsukami. That action belongs to the same chain of failed alternatives and deliberate power grabs. Itachi did not create the discrimination, coup plan or Danzō's methods. He did, however, make the fatal decision to act within the options presented. Responsibility is therefore distributed without disappearing: the village created conditions, the conspirators chose a dangerous plan, Danzō forced an atrocious solution and Itachi carried it out.

Why did Itachi choose the village instead of the clan?

Official Naruto material traces Itachi's aversion to war to childhood experiences of mass death. When Fugaku exposes him to the aftermath of the Third Great Ninja War, Itachi develops a deep suspicion of any claim that mass killing can be a clean path to victory. His desire for peace is genuine. The contradiction is that he eventually commits mass killing in the name of preventing more of it. Naruto presents that contradiction rather than a neat rule that says a noble objective makes every method acceptable.

Itachi also sees the potential for one internal conflict to widen. An unstable Leaf Village could become vulnerable to outside powers and draw more people into violence. The story gives readers reasons to understand why a gifted but very young shinobi might view the choice as catastrophic either way. Understanding his fear does not require accepting that a coup would certainly have escalated in exactly the way he imagined. Characters in desperate situations make forecasts, and their forecasts are not identical to guaranteed futures.

Why was Sasuke the one person he spared?

Sasuke is not a minor exception in Itachi's plan; he is the emotional center of it. Itachi wants his younger brother to survive, grow strong and eventually confront the apparent villain responsible for his suffering. That gives Sasuke a target for his grief while keeping the political details hidden. From Itachi's perspective, taking the entire burden onto himself lets Sasuke keep living. From Sasuke's perspective, the choice destroys his family and gives his childhood a single violent purpose.

This is the uncomfortable distinction behind the question 'Did Itachi do it for Sasuke?' In part, yes. But love and control can exist together, and Itachi attempts to control Sasuke's future through secrecy, hatred and planned confrontation. Sasuke does not consent to having his life shaped that way. After learning the truth, he does not simply forgive the village or become the man Itachi imagined. He directs his anger toward a different source. The attempt to protect him has created new trauma rather than resolving the old.

Did Itachi work alone?

The political story involves the masked Uchiha figure later known as Obito, who helps carry out the slaughter. This is another reason to avoid a simplistic account that portrays every moment as a lone boy defeating an entire clan in one ordinary battle. The eventual revelation concerns a coordinated atrocity enabled by secrecy, surprise and extraordinary abilities. Itachi is central and accountable, but the larger conspiracy matters to how it became possible.

People sometimes ask whether he killed his own parents. The canonical tragedy includes Fugaku and Mikoto among the victims, and the official Itachi retrospective discusses the agony of their final encounter. That scene matters because the narrative refuses to make the massacre abstract. The people dying are not just symbols of factional politics. They are parents, friends and civilians who have different degrees of responsibility for the approaching coup. The difference between targeting an armed conspiracy and destroying a population is morally enormous.

The lie Itachi chose to live with

Itachi leaves Konoha as a missing-nin and joins Akatsuki, ensuring that Sasuke and much of the world will see him as a criminal. His later encounters are therefore performed through several layers: an actual dangerous operative, a brother presenting himself as monstrous, and a secret bearer waiting for the truth to die with him. The narrative does not ask the viewer to assume every cruel word is secretly kind. Some of the damage he causes Sasuke is unmistakably real.

Consider how much Itachi requires the lie to accomplish. Sasuke must hate him, keep pursuing power and believe that defeating him will settle their family's past. That is a tall demand to place on a child. When the secret is revealed after the brothers' fight, the story changes genre for Sasuke: what he thought was private revenge becomes a political accusation against the village. The 'villain' revelation solves a mystery about motives while opening a harder question about whether institutions should survive the acts they commit to protect themselves.

What the official account establishes and what it doesn't

We can establish that the Uchiha planned a coup, Itachi was caught between clan and village, Danzō applied pressure, Shisui's intervention failed after his eye was taken, Sasuke was deliberately spared and the village kept the operation secret. Naruto's official birthday column and the publisher's Itachi novel overview are unusually useful here because they attach the explanation to recognizable manga chapters and published supplementary stories.

What canon does not establish is a mathematical proof that murdering the clan was the only possible way to avoid civil war. A character's claim of necessity is part of the drama, not a replacement for moral judgment. Nor does the revelation retroactively cancel the abuse of Sasuke or the rights of the people killed. A trustworthy guide should preserve those boundaries rather than copy social-media slogans calling the massacre a self-evident act of heroism.

A better way to remember the truth: four separate questions

Who created the political crisis? Long-standing suspicion and clan–village hostility helped produce an environment in which the coup became attractive to some leaders. Who chose to rebel? The Uchiha faction preparing an armed seizure of power carries its own responsibility. Who pushed the extermination plan? Danzō and the hardline security response are central. Who actually carried out the massacre? Itachi, with help from the masked man. These are different questions, so the answer to one should never erase the others.

And who paid? Sasuke did, but not alone. The dead Uchiha are often absent from fan arguments because the narrative follows the living brother. It is still important to keep them present. This is what makes Itachi such an enduring figure: he embodies a world in which talented young people are trained to accept horrifying acts as duty. Seeing his pain should sharpen our judgment of the system around him, not make us forget the suffering that system inflicted.

Questions readers often ask

Did Itachi hate the Uchiha? His opposition to the coup does not mean he simply hated his relatives. The official narrative repeatedly stresses the conflict between duty and attachment. Was Danzō responsible? His intervention was decisive, but a complete account includes the village's deeper failures and Itachi's agency. Why didn't Itachi tell Sasuke the truth immediately? He hoped Sasuke could survive outside the political burden and define himself against a single villain, a plan that ultimately damaged him.

Did Sasuke ever learn the truth? Yes, and his reaction changes the direction of Naruto Shippuden. Did Itachi become a hero afterward? That is an interpretation, not an acquittal delivered by the story's world. If you are reading the manga, the official Itachi retrospective cites Naruto chapters 400, 403 and 590 among its key evidence points. Those are more useful anchors than a single decontextualized viral clip.
