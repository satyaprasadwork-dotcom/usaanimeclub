Shiba Inu Rooms TV Anime Announced for Summer 2027 With First Cast

The supernatural comedy Shiba Inu Rooms is getting a TV anime in summer 2027, with a teaser and initial cast and staff announced.

桜
USA Anime Club Editorial

Published November 14, 2026 · Updated November 14, 2026

THE STORY AT A GLANCE

The official adaptation announcement gives Shiba Inu Rooms a summer 2027 Japanese broadcast window, a teaser and initial cast including Niina Nakabayashi and Mutsumi Tamura.

A new adaptation on the schedule

The official Shiba Inu Rooms accounts announced on November 1 that Esu Omori's supernatural comedy manga is being adapted into a television anime for a summer 2027 broadcast in Japan. The announcement was accompanied by a teaser trailer, key visual and initial cast and staff information.

The anime project's official English website identifies TMS Entertainment as the animation studio and Osamu Yamasaki as director. It names Niina Nakabayashi as Kori Momose and Mutsumi Tamura as Mu, the Shiba Inu spirit at the center of the series. These are confirmed production details, not a US broadcast commitment.

What makes the premise distinctive

The story follows student Kori as she moves into an apartment occupied by Mu, an earthbound Shiba Inu spirit. Their awkward attempts at coexistence supply the supernatural-comedy premise. The original manga began as a Shonen Jump+ one-shot and entered serialization in 2024, with VIZ handling English manga publication.

The adaptation may interest readers who prefer gentler character comedy to action-centered fantasy, but it is too early to judge the series' pacing or final visual execution from an announcement trailer alone. Promotional footage and finished episodes can differ.

The US distribution question

The confirmed window is a Japanese summer 2027 broadcast. A statement about Japanese television does not establish a North American simulcast, English-dub cast, or premiere time on a US platform. None of those should be derived from the fact that English-language anime websites are covering the news.

A subsequent US licensing announcement could establish the platform, territory and release schedule still missing here. In the meantime, the published teaser and staff credits are the reliable details worth following, while VIZ's English manga provides a legally published path into the original story.

What was revealed with the first adaptation announcement

The November 1 report on Shiba Inu Rooms identifies a summer 2027 Japanese television window, a teaser, a visual and named members of the production. The story comes from Esu Omori's supernatural-comedy manga and centers on a student who shares living space with a dog spirit. That is already enough to establish a real anime project rather than a piece of fan art or a hypothetical adaptation pitch. But the distinction between a Japanese broadcast window and a US streaming license remains essential; the cited announcement does not name an American platform.

The production's official English-language website identifies TMS Entertainment with the production and Osamu Yamasaki as director. It also names Niina Nakabayashi for Kori Momose and Mutsumi Tamura for Mu, the Shiba Inu spirit. Readers can use these credits as identifiers when later promotional materials appear. Those are formal roles attached to this adaptation, not a guarantee that every scene will resemble a studio's prior series or that a cast member will appear in all episodes. Additional character announcements, if any, should be verified separately.

The teaser is useful as an early signal of character design and tone, but it is still promotional material. A short clip cannot resolve a series' episode count, final pacing or animation consistency. Likewise, a visual can include a specific character pairing without proving the full cast roster is complete. Good release reporting records which facts are attached to the teaser rather than building a speculative production schedule from brief footage. At this stage, summer 2027 is a broad window covering several possible Japanese broadcast start dates, not an exact US premiere.

Why a haunted apartment and a dog spirit make an unusual starting point

The premise sends Kori Momose into an apartment occupied by Mu, a Shiba Inu spirit who remains attached to the property. Rather than beginning with a fantasy battlefield, the story uses an everyday housing arrangement as the place where the supernatural intrudes. That gives the comedy a concrete problem: two beings with different expectations have to share a home. The setup can invite humor from territorial habits, misunderstandings and reluctant companionship, but the official synopsis is not enough to declare what kinds of jokes or emotional developments dominate later chapters.

The animal-spirit element is notable because Shiba Inus are readily recognizable even outside Japan. Yet Mu is a fictional character rather than a real pet, and the show should not be understood as a documentary about dog behavior. A reader may enjoy the visual association and the idea of a canine ghost while still caring about Kori's perspective as a teenager navigating a new environment. This everyday-supernatural combination can create an accessible entry point for viewers who usually skip battle-oriented adaptations, though that is an assessment of premise rather than an advance review.

English-language readers have another route to the story because VIZ is associated with the manga's English publication. The original Japanese serialization history can help establish that the adaptation has a source to draw from, but it does not reveal the number of chapters a first anime season will cover. A short teaser may foreground the most immediately recognizable relationship and omit other story threads entirely. Anyone trying to predict the adaptation's ending from an announcement should distinguish the source material's ongoing publication from the television production's still-unannounced episode structure.

The specific questions US fans should follow next

First is a verifiable American distributor announcement. A fan-oriented English-language article about a Japanese broadcast does not prove that the publication carrying it will eventually stream the show. Even if a service distributes other TMS Entertainment works, rights are licensed title by title and territory by territory. The relevant evidence would identify the platform and the region, ideally with a dated schedule. Until that appears, naming a US app as the definitive home would be guesswork. Our original article deliberately leaves that information open.

Second are the precise broadcast date and localization details. Summer 2027 narrows the Japanese release season but does not supply the day, hour, American subtitle availability or dub production information. Someone planning to watch weekly needs more than a promotional season. Fans should treat screenshots containing unsourced release dates cautiously and check the official adaptation account or a verified distributor listing as the date approaches. The same goes for voice-actor lists: the named initial pair is confirmed, while any additional casting requires separate credit.

Third is the eventual adaptation itself. The premise suggests warmth and situational comedy, but final judgments about direction, writing and pacing belong after actual episodes are available. The official teaser and named staff provide a genuine reason for interest without requiring invented excitement or exaggerated certainty. The official anime website confirms the summer broadcast window without naming a US platform. That distinction keeps the story useful whether readers come to it today or discover it months later.
