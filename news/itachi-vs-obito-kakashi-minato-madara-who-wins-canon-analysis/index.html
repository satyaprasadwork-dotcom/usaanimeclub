Itachi vs Obito, Kakashi, Minato and Madara: Who Wins Each Fight?

Itachi vs Obito, Kakashi, Minato and Madara: four Naruto matchups explained through canon encounters, Kamui, Tsukuyomi, Flying Raijin, Susanoo and character versions.

桜
USA Anime Club Editorial

Editor-in-Chief: Satya Prasad

Published November 15, 2026 · Updated November 15, 2026

QUICK ANSWER

Early Itachi decisively incapacitates Kakashi with Tsukuyomi in their canon encounter. Itachi has no definitive canonical one-on-one duel against Obito, Minato or Madara. In specific hypothetical versions, we lean masked Obito for Kamui, living Minato for Flying Raijin's positional control, and war-era Madara for overwhelming scale. Later Kakashi forms must be compared separately from the Kakashi Itachi actually faced.

Four Itachi matchups, but not four canon fights

What makes Itachi-versus debates so enduring is that his greatest weapon is rarely the most visible one. He can end a confrontation before his opponent understands what was real. Put that approach against Obito's intangibility, Kakashi's evolving Sharingan, Minato's teleportation or Madara's overwhelming battlefield power, and there is no single rule that settles every encounter. Saying Itachi wins because he is a genius explains too little. So does saying he loses because the other character looks faster or has a larger Susanoo.

There is a crucial factual distinction at the beginning. Itachi and Kakashi actually confront one another, and their early encounter ends with Kakashi incapacitated by Tsukuyomi. The series does not provide equivalent completed, decisive one-on-one battles between Itachi and Obito, Minato or Madara. Those three are hypothetical comparisons based on demonstrated techniques. This guide separates what the manga and anime show from what we can reasonably infer. Major Naruto Shippuden spoilers follow, including revelations about the Uchiha, reanimation and the Fourth Great Ninja War.

The answer in brief: our conditional verdicts

Against the masked Obito, we lean toward Obito in an unprepared fight. Kamui gives him an unusual ability to deny physical contact, making Itachi's strongest finishing options difficult to deliver. Itachi's tactical intelligence and genjutsu leave room for an upset if he understands the technique and prepares a specific trap. Against early-series Kakashi, there is no need to speculate about the recorded encounter: Itachi's Tsukuyomi wins the exchange. Later war Kakashi is a different question, and the brief double-Mangekyo version from the Kaguya fight is different again.

Against living Fourth Hokage Minato, our judgment leans toward Minato because Flying Raijin can control position and timing while Itachi's ill body cannot sustain an endless exchange. Itachi still has real routes through a carefully applied illusion or sealing strike. Against reanimated war-era Madara, the evidence strongly favors Madara, whose clones, battlefield-scale attacks and Perfect Susanoo surpass Itachi's demonstrated output. The Ten-Tails version creates an even wider mismatch. These are editorial assessments, not odds calculated by the creator or official rankings issued by the franchise.

Before comparing anyone, specify the character version

Naruto has an unusual habit of changing the terms of a matchup late in the story. The Itachi who fights Sasuke is seriously ill and physically deteriorating; the Itachi brought back through Edo Tensei no longer faces those limits in the same way. Kakashi first faces Itachi long before he learns to use Kamui as effectively as he does during the war. His final temporary double-Mangekyo power is not something he can draw on throughout his career. A fair analysis cannot grant him those abilities in his earlier encounter and claim to be describing canon.

The same warning applies to Obito and Madara. Masked Obito's use of Kamui is not equivalent to the Ten-Tails jinchuriki's capabilities. Reanimated Madara's performance against the Five Kage is not a mere preview of a conventional young Uchiha with the same health and equipment as Itachi. Minato also has an ordinary living form and a later reanimated form with access to Kurama's chakra. When a fan discussion combines every person's best moment without declaring these changes, it creates an imaginary character on each side. That is a fun game, perhaps, but it should not be mistaken for evidence.

Itachi's toolkit: more than a list of powerful eye techniques

Itachi's advantage begins with information. His Sharingan lets him read and anticipate movements, while his genjutsu can make an opponent waste a critical moment responding to something that did not happen. Tsukuyomi punishes particular visual engagement with intense mental suffering. Amaterasu brings dangerous black flames into the fight. Susanoo adds protection, and its Totsuka Blade gives him a sealing option. The Yata Mirror is associated with that defensive arsenal. These abilities work differently, but they share a theme: Itachi wants to create a moment when the opponent's safest-looking choice is actually the mistake.

His strengths come with conditions. Illness limits the living Itachi of the later story, and using powerful Mangekyo techniques is physically demanding. Izanami requires a particular setup and sacrifices sight in the eye used; the war-arc fight with Kabuto is not proof that it can defeat any person on demand. Kotoamatsukami originated with Shisui's preserved eye and a carefully arranged crow contingency, rather than being a native attack that Itachi can cast freely every few seconds. Give Itachi his real abilities and he remains dangerous enough. He does not need powers the story never shows him possessing.

Itachi vs Obito: Kamui changes the meaning of an attack

A conventional fighter can usually be threatened by a sufficiently fast or well-timed strike. Obito makes that assumption unreliable. His Kamui can shift relevant parts of his body through a connected time-space dimension, letting attacks appear to pass through him. The official Naruto war retrospective describes the alliance struggling with this defense until Kakashi recognizes how his own Kamui interacts with it. Their coordinated attacks then reveal a genuine opening. This proves Obito's technique is formidable, not that his body exists beyond all possible harm.

For Itachi, the challenge is to create the moment Obito has to become tangible. An Amaterasu cast, a shuriken feint or a Susanoo attack may all demand a response, but merely launching the technique is not enough. Itachi would have to anticipate where the target will be, what action forces physical interaction and whether an evasion route is still available. That is an interesting tactical puzzle for a precise fighter. Obito, meanwhile, can choose to withdraw and make a sick opponent spend resources trying to create an opening that may never appear. Those asymmetrical demands are why we lean Obito in a first encounter.

Itachi vs Obito: could Tsukuyomi beat him?

A common argument says Itachi only needs to meet Obito's eyes. This skips the most difficult question: does that valid encounter occur under conditions where the illusion is successfully applied? Obito has extensive Sharingan experience and uses deception as an everyday tool. That does not make him officially immune to Tsukuyomi. But Itachi has never demonstrated a successful Tsukuyomi against Obito in a completed canon duel either. Both universal claims would go further than the source material allows.

One scenario favors Itachi more than a random brawl: he knows the opponent's technique and engineers an apparent safe opportunity for Obito to look or move in the wrong way. Another favors Obito: he has advance knowledge of Itachi's genjutsu, refuses predictable visual engagement and makes his opponent chase a body that repeatedly vanishes. This is a contest about information as much as chakra. The fact that both fighters are intelligent Uchiha does not settle whose deception works first. We can identify the risk without inventing a definitive unseen exchange.

Itachi vs Obito: Amaterasu and the Totsuka Blade

Amaterasu would be dangerous if its flames reached Obito at a vulnerable instant, and using the fire to constrain space might help Itachi read an evasion pattern. The black flames are not, however, documented as a universal switch that permanently disables Kamui. The same care is needed when discussing the Totsuka Blade. Its sealing effect could be devastating if delivered, but a lethal effect is not evidence that contact is guaranteed. Obito's very best defensive quality is denying that contact.

Itachi's Susanoo might protect him from a sudden approach, but making his own position obvious can also help an opponent who can disengage. Obito must become vulnerable at certain moments to perform particular physical actions; Itachi might exploit one of them. Yet knowing that a window exists and successfully hitting it are different achievements. The narrative never stages a clean contest between these weapons and Obito's complete kit. Our verdict therefore recognizes a plausible Itachi ambush or trap without assuming he executes it whenever asked.

Itachi vs Obito: the version-dependent verdict

For living, late-life Itachi versus masked Obito, both entering without preparation, Obito has the clearer route to controlling the pace. He can make Itachi prove that an attack will connect while he looks for the moment to capture or punish the opponent. This does not mean Obito would defeat Itachi instantly or that every interaction would favor him. It means the defensive problem he presents is particularly difficult for someone who has limited time and must commit to costly techniques.

If Itachi knows exactly how Kamui's dimensional interaction works and has enough time to arrange the battlefield, his prospects improve. If instead the comparison uses Ten-Tails Obito, the argument changes dramatically: the official war narrative documents power, endurance and defenses beyond the masked man's ordinary state. In that later matchup, we would strongly favor Obito. Anyone giving a single final percentage for every incarnation of both characters is hiding the very assumptions that determine the outcome.

Itachi vs Kakashi: this fight really happened

The original Naruto story includes the decisive evidence. Itachi and Kisame appear in the Leaf and face shinobi including Asuma, Kurenai and Kakashi. Kakashi joins the confrontation, only to suffer Itachi's Mangekyo technique Tsukuyomi. The official Naruto twentieth-anniversary retrospective describes the attack and Kakashi's mental ordeal. The original anime directory identifies their encounter in episode 82, Eye to Eye, Sharingan vs. Sharingan. The confirmed result is that Itachi overwhelms Kakashi in that critical genjutsu exchange.

This encounter makes a specific point about the Sharingan. Possessing an eye of the same family is not automatic immunity from an advanced visual technique. Kakashi is exceptionally skilled, yet he cannot safely handle what Itachi subjects him to in that moment. At the same time, this is not a contest between every form the two shinobi will ever possess. Kakashi continues growing through Shippuden, and later powers change the methods available to him. We should respect the actual result without extending it into a rule that future Kakashi must always lose the same way.

Itachi vs Kakashi: does war-era Kamui change the answer?

War-era Kakashi has a more developed spatial option. During the confrontation with Obito, he helps identify that the two Kamui abilities lead to the same dimension, then contributes to a coordinated counter. The official war summary shows the team turning a seemingly untouchable opponent into one they can hit. That feat combines observation, spatial technique and timing. It would give late Kakashi an entirely different way to threaten Itachi compared with their first encounter, which turned on visual genjutsu.

There is an important limitation: Kamui is taxing. The same official recap notes Kakashi exhausting substantial chakra through repeated use and needing help to continue. A late-war Itachi versus Kakashi fight might therefore come down to which one secures a decisive application before paying an unsustainable cost. Can Kakashi get an accurate Kamui attack without offering a Tsukuyomi opportunity? Can Itachi impose a convincing illusion before Kakashi attacks through the spatial route? Those are useful hypothetical questions, but neither has an official on-screen answer.

Itachi vs Kakashi: what about DMS Kakashi?

Near the finale, Kakashi receives a brief, extraordinary gift connected to Obito's Mangekyo powers and fights beside Team 7 against Kaguya. The official Naruto finale recap documents his use of Susanoo and Kamui-enhanced techniques. Fans call this temporary state DMS Kakashi. It is important to remember that he does not maintain those powers as his normal permanent equipment. Using them to rewrite what happened during his first meeting with Itachi would be a mistake about the timeline.

If the comparison explicitly allows temporary double-Mangekyo Kakashi against living Itachi, we favor Kakashi. The available offensive and defensive options have changed too drastically to treat this as a replay of the early Tsukuyomi encounter. This is a hypothetical judgment, not a canon rematch. The defensible three-part summary is straightforward: early Kakashi lost the real genjutsu encounter; ordinary war Kakashi presents a more complicated hypothetical; the exceptionally empowered temporary DMS version has the stronger hypothetical position.

Why Itachi and Kakashi are such interesting opponents

Both are patient observers, but they use information differently. Kakashi reads the situation, learns from others and coordinates unconventional solutions. Itachi prefers to control what the opponent believes before the fight's decisive move. Their early exchange demonstrates the cost of letting Itachi establish that control. Their hypothetical later meeting would ask whether Kakashi could reverse the information advantage using his knowledge of ocular techniques, battlefield positioning and Kamui.

A comparison framed solely as whose Sharingan is stronger misses the emotional shape of Naruto's writing. Kakashi becomes a teacher partly because he knows the cost of loss and secrecy. Itachi repeatedly keeps the truth from the people closest to him. The two embody very different ways of handling painful knowledge. That does not decide a fight, but it offers readers a more distinctive reason to revisit their scenes than a recycled ranking of jutsu names.

Itachi vs Minato: why the opening second matters

Itachi and the Fourth Hokage do not have a canon one-on-one duel settling their superiority. Their abilities nevertheless make a compelling hypothetical. Minato's Flying Raijin teleports him to marked points, allowing him to change position without running across the intervening space. The Naruto official database explains how the marks work and distinguishes Minato's virtually unrestricted use of the technique from limitations placed on a later adaptation. Movement, positioning and immediate attack timing are major parts of his combat identity.

Itachi would normally try to shape an opponent's perception of range, line of sight and the next attack. Minato can abandon a location almost instantaneously, which complicates those expectations. Yet teleportation is not clairvoyance. Minato must choose where to place markers and when to act, and he can still make wrong assessments. A smart Itachi might exploit the expectation that a particular marker is safe. A smart Minato might refuse to let Itachi establish the eye-contact or sealing window in the first place.

Itachi vs Minato: the masked-man encounter is relevant, not decisive

During the Nine-Tails attack, Minato confronts the masked man and finds a way to strike at the correct moment despite the opponent's space-time technique. The official Naruto retrospective describes him breaking the masked figure's control and forcing a retreat. That is excellent evidence for Minato's ability to identify a vulnerability quickly, not evidence that he has already defeated Itachi. Obito's technique and Itachi's techniques are different problems.

This distinction protects the argument from an easy but false syllogism. Minato counters the masked man, therefore he must be immune to Tsukuyomi: that conclusion does not follow. The scene instead shows why Minato is a strong tactical candidate. He can read a dangerous encounter, time a teleportation and seize the instant in which an attack is possible. Itachi has similar strengths in deception and anticipation. A hypothetical clash would be a contest over whose interpretation of the opening is right.

Itachi vs Minato: can teleportation escape genjutsu?

Speed does not automatically prevent a mental attack once its relevant conditions have been met. Itachi's Tsukuyomi is not a conventional projectile that Minato simply outruns. Equally, merely possessing the technique does not establish that Itachi will land it against someone who can change positions abruptly and understands the importance of dangerous eye contact. The story does not provide a laboratory-style test of Minato against Itachi's Mangekyo, so confident claims of instant success or perfect immunity are speculative.

For a useful comparison, imagine both fighters with no advance information. Minato probes the distance while Itachi tries to identify the pattern behind sudden movement. Now give both knowledge. Minato becomes more cautious about visual engagement; Itachi may look for a predictable marker or apparent escape route to exploit. Neither scenario changes their fundamental techniques, but it changes their likely behavior. That is why intelligence and preparation should be stated rather than silently granted to whichever character a writer wants to win.

Itachi vs Minato: Rasengan, Susanoo and stamina

Minato can deliver Rasengan after a well-chosen teleport, forcing the defender to respond with remarkable speed. Itachi might use an illusion or Susanoo, especially if he knows an attack is coming. But activating an impressive defense does not automatically let him hold a teleporting opponent in place. Minato can withdraw, reposition and search for another angle. Amaterasu might make parts of the arena dangerous, yet Flying Raijin can reduce dependence on an ordinary route through the fire.

The duration of the fight is important. Living Itachi's health is explicitly compromised by the time he faces Sasuke. A long contest with repeated high-cost Mangekyo techniques works against him. Minato still has to expend attention and chakra, but his ability to choose when the fighters come into direct contact gives him a structural advantage in a standard encounter. Itachi's best hope may be to make a particular moment look safe and end the fight there. This is why we favor Minato without denying that a well-timed Itachi trap could succeed.

Itachi vs Minato: our conditional verdict

For living Fourth Hokage Minato versus living, illness-affected Itachi, we lean Minato because Flying Raijin repeatedly challenges Itachi's control of spacing and timing. Minato's response to the masked man supports that assessment with a demonstrated example. That is not a claim of absolute genjutsu immunity or that Minato would never lose a tactical exchange. It is a judgment about which character can more consistently enforce the sort of fight he wants.

If Itachi is reanimated, ordinary stamina and injury assumptions change. If Minato is given his later reanimated Kurama-enhanced abilities, the comparison shifts in another direction. If either fighter has arranged the battlefield in advance, markers and traps become more important than raw speed rhetoric. We would not describe those cases as the same fight. A verdict is useful only when a reader can see the conditions that produced it.

Itachi vs Madara: the largest difference in demonstrated power

Madara is the clearest unfavorable matchup for Itachi once we use his demonstrated war-era form. The official Naruto Madara retrospective shows him fighting large Allied Shinobi Forces formations with powerful fire style, Susanoo, Rinnegan and destructive meteorites. In the Five Kage confrontation, he uses twenty-five wood clones capable of employing Susanoo, then manifests the tremendous Perfect Susanoo. These are actual story events, not numbers fabricated by a fan power chart.

Itachi is an exceptionally dangerous single-combat tactician, but being able to outthink one opponent does not answer multiple capable clones and large-scale assaults at the same time. Both men are Uchiha and both possess Susanoo, yet those shared labels do not mean their shown output is equal. Madara can impose a level of battlefield pressure that makes Itachi's careful attempts to arrange one decisive opening far more difficult. A good analysis should acknowledge that difference before raising unusual possible counters.

Itachi vs Madara: would Tsukuyomi be enough?

Tsukuyomi is still a serious threat, and readers are justified in asking whether Madara could be caught by an extraordinary visual illusion. The manga, however, never shows a completed duel where Itachi applies it successfully to Madara. Madara is a highly experienced Uchiha ocular-jutsu user; assuming he would casually walk into an unknown eye trap is not a strong basis for a verdict. Nor can we declare a universal immunity without evidence of the exact interaction.

Even a plausible illusion would have to be applied before Madara's scale changes the battle. Against an opponent who can attack through clones and massive destructive techniques, Itachi may not get the controlled one-on-one visual exchange he prefers. A possible effect under ideal conditions is different from a likely result under typical ones. This is the main analytical distinction: Itachi has tools that can cause serious consequences, but Madara has more ways to prevent him from reaching the point where those tools can be used decisively.

Itachi vs Madara: could the Totsuka Blade seal him?

The Totsuka Blade is the most interesting route to a possible upset. Its sealing power could matter greatly against a reanimated enemy because ordinary damage is not always a permanent solution. Yet an attack having a decisive effect when it lands tells us nothing about whether it can reach this particular opponent. Itachi would need to deliver a strike through Madara's offense and defenses, potentially while distinguishing the real Madara from multiple dangerous clones.

The war-arc evidence makes that obstacle enormous. Madara's Susanoo-capable wood clones and Perfect Susanoo show how much space and pressure he can control. We cannot claim Itachi's blade would automatically pass through all of that, just as we cannot dismiss sealing as irrelevant in principle. Our view is that a hypothetical Itachi victory requires a very specific and unshown contact scenario, while Madara has numerous demonstrated paths to pressure or defeat him. That strongly favors Madara without erasing the theoretical threat.

Itachi vs Madara: living, Edo Tensei and Ten-Tails forms

Madara's power changes substantially across the story. Historical living Madara has immense experience and extraordinary Uchiha abilities. Reanimated Madara demonstrates the arsenal that overwhelms the Allied Shinobi Forces and Kage. Later he becomes the Ten-Tails jinchuriki and faces opponents empowered by the Sage of Six Paths. These are not minor costume changes. They alter his endurance, scale and possible defenses in ways that must be specified when comparing him with another fighter.

Against war-era reanimated Madara, we strongly favor Madara. Against Ten-Tails Madara, the gap grows even larger. Restricting the comparison to some earlier Madara version may produce a more nuanced conversation, but it cannot legitimately borrow feats from his final transformation while denying that the transformation is being used. The same fairness applies to Itachi: giving him an immortal reanimated body requires saying so openly. The two characters can both be admired without pretending that their strongest demonstrated battlefields are equivalent.

Our four-matchup scorecard, without invented odds

Itachi versus masked Obito: no definitive canon duel; conditional edge to Obito because Kamui prevents reliable contact, with a prepared Itachi potentially able to exploit a forced opening. Itachi versus Kakashi: early direct encounter won by Itachi's Tsukuyomi. War-era Kakashi: unresolved hypothetical, with Kamui providing serious counterplay. Temporary DMS Kakashi: conditional edge to Kakashi in that exceptional late-war form.

Itachi versus living Minato: no definitive canon duel; conditional edge to Minato for Flying Raijin's control of distance, while Itachi remains dangerous through genjutsu and a sealing hit. Itachi versus reanimated or Ten-Tails Madara: no definitive canon duel; strong advantage to Madara because his documented resources and scale dwarf those seen in Itachi's ordinary fights. We avoid imaginary ninety-nine-percent predictions. The evidence supports broad leanings more readily than exact probabilities.

Three scenarios that make the debate more useful

First is an unprepared meeting. Both characters recognize the other as dangerous but do not know all the details of their opponent's abilities. That gives unusual importance to the first encounter and the ability to learn without being punished. Second is a prepared duel in which both know the relevant techniques and can position themselves. Itachi can design misleading openings, Minato can distribute marks and Obito can plan an approach. The third is a mission with a defined objective: capture, seal, survive or force a retreat rather than simply kill.

A reader can make the comparison more rigorous by writing down the character versions, starting distance, preparation, available outside assistance and what counts as victory. If the explanation depends on Obito voluntarily standing still, Kakashi ignoring an obvious illusion, Minato forgetting his markers or Madara refusing to use his clones, the scenario may be doing all the work. A good hypothetical should allow both fighters to behave intelligently, not only the reader's favorite.

Five familiar claims that need more care

The first is that Sharingan ownership gives universal Tsukuyomi immunity; Kakashi's early encounter shows that eye users can have radically different capabilities. The second is that Kamui makes Obito eternally untouchable, when the official war story documents a dimensional interaction and an opening. The third is that Minato's teleportation automatically protects his mind. The fourth is that the Totsuka Blade wins as soon as someone mentions it, ignoring the challenge of getting a sealing strike to land.

The fifth is that the word Susanoo makes Itachi and Madara equals, regardless of size, clones and other techniques. An additional problem is the healthy Itachi hypothetical. Removing his illness can be an interesting thought experiment, but the manga never displays a fully documented alternate career in which his chakra, stamina and every battle outcome can be measured. Calling that form canon does not make the argument stronger. Naruto rewards careful attention to conditions, even when fans would prefer a simpler result.

Why these matchups reveal different sides of Itachi

Obito tests Itachi's ability to force contact against someone who can appear to leave the physical world. Kakashi tests whether learning and experience can reverse an earlier defeat. Minato tests the value of choosing the precise instant and location of an exchange. Madara tests how far cleverness can go when an opponent commands overwhelming area-wide resources. Each pairing therefore tells readers something different about fighting styles, rather than providing four excuses to repeat that Itachi has a sealing sword.

There is a character story beneath the techniques, too. Itachi spends much of his life controlling what others believe and making plans for how they should act. Naruto reveals the terrible limits of that approach through Sasuke's response to the truth. His best hypothetical matchups are similarly interesting when the plan can fail. He might read a habit brilliantly, misjudge a teleportation or create an elegant trap that an opponent refuses to enter. Intelligent fighters remain fallible, and admitting that makes the debate more faithful to the source material.

Frequently asked questions about all four fights

Did Itachi defeat Kakashi? Yes, in their early encounter he incapacitates Kakashi with Tsukuyomi. Did Itachi ever beat Obito, Minato or Madara in a definitive canonical duel? No such completed one-on-one result establishes those wins. Does DMS Kakashi mean Itachi's earlier victory never mattered? No; that is a later temporary form that changes the terms. Could Itachi catch Obito in Tsukuyomi? It is a possible hypothetical opening, not a demonstrated canonical result against Obito. Could Minato teleport out of Tsukuyomi? His teleportation does not by itself establish immunity to an illusion once applied.

Would a perfectly healthy Itachi beat Madara? The story does not provide a fully shown healthy endgame Itachi with measurable alternate feats, and late-war Madara still has a commanding display of power. Could Itachi's Totsuka Blade seal an opponent who is already reanimated? Its sealing role is relevant, but successful contact remains the issue in a hypothetical. Which pairing has the strongest source-backed answer? Itachi versus early Kakashi, because Naruto actually depicts the decisive genjutsu exchange. Why are the other conclusions conditional? Because one missing battle cannot be made into a recorded fact by confidently imagining it.

How these conclusions were checked

We used Naruto's official retrospective of Itachi, the original anime episode listing for the Kakashi encounter, the Great Ninja War accounts of Kamui and the Five Kage battle, official explanations of Flying Raijin, and official coverage of Madara's transformations and temporary double-Mangekyo Kakashi. The retrospectives connect much of the discussion to recognizable original manga chapters, which can be checked through authorized editions such as VIZ's English Naruto catalog.

Those official materials establish the techniques and events. They do not declare official winners for the three unshown duels or for Kakashi's alternative later forms. Our verdicts are editorial interpretations of what the techniques make possible, with uncertainty wherever the franchise leaves an interaction unresolved. If you want to challenge one, begin by naming the exact version, the battlefield and the mechanism of your preferred winning move. That makes a better discussion than a ranking copied from a thumbnail.
